## Verified facts (use ONLY these numbers)

### Reading rate - Brysbaert (2019), J. Memory and Language 109, 104047 - tier A
- 190 studies, 18,573 participants. Silent reading, adults, English: **238 wpm non-fiction, 260 fiction**.
- Most adults: **175-300 wpm** non-fiction; **200-320** fiction. Oral reading **183 wpm** (77 studies, 5,965 people).
- Non-fiction slower mainly because of longer words. Valid for adults 18-60 without reading problems.
- The **300 wpm** figure "is widely mentioned"; a 300 target is "unrealistic for the majority of people".
- Skimming is a different activity; 40,000 newspaper words in an hour "reads" at 667 wpm only arithmetically.
- RSVP study he cites (Benedetto et al. 2015): ~200 wpm, similar to paper, worse comprehension.

### Speed reading - Rayner, Schotter, Masson, Potter, Treiman (2016), PSPI 17(1) 4-34 - tier A
- Good college-educated readers **200-400 wpm**; "a normal pace for most readers is 200 to 400 wpm".
- "There is a trade-off between speed and accuracy." Doubling or tripling (250 to 500-750) with equal understanding is "unlikely".
- Speed reading or skimming gets you through faster "with moderate comprehension".
- Skimming can be **two to four times** faster than typical silent reading, with lower comprehension.
- Limit = language processing / word identification, not eye control; "limited by our ability to attend to, identify, and understand words rather than our ability to see them".
- Inner speech: suppression impairs comprehension on reasonably difficult text; "not a nuisance activity that must be eliminated". RSVP at 720 wpm readers still generate sound-based codes (Petrick 1981, as cited).
- Regressions are more often the repair than the cause of a misunderstanding.
- Reported in the review (tier R, originals not read): Calef, Pieper and Coffey 1999 ~280 to ~400 wpm, comprehension 81% to 74%; Just, Masson and Carpenter 1980 speed readers 600-700 wpm behaved like skimmers, gist advantage vanished on Scientific American texts; Liddle 1965; Homa 1983; Benedetto et al. 2015 Spritz 200 vs 209 wpm, more eye fatigue, poorer literal comprehension; Hyönä, Lorch and Kaakinen 2002 heading-attenders wrote the best summaries; Duggan and Payne 2009 skimming whole text beat reading half normally for important ideas.
- Effective speed readers were "intelligent people who already know a great deal concerning the topic".
- Route to faster reading: practice with language, vocabulary, reading more and varied texts. Comfortable speaking rate 150-160 wpm.

### Learning techniques - Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan, Willingham (2013), PSPI 14(1) 4-58 - tier A
- **High:** practice testing, distributed practice. **Moderate:** elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaved practice. **Low:** summarization, highlighting/underlining, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text learning, rereading.
- "Most students report rereading and highlighting, yet these techniques do not consistently boost students' performance". Moderate ones fell short because evidence "is limited".
- Spaced rereading consistently outperforms massed rereading on delayed tests (p.28-29).

### Testing effect - Roediger and Karpicke (2006), Psych Science 17(3) 249-255 - tier A
- Exp 1: 120 students, two prose passages (256 and 275 words), restudy vs test. **5 min: restudy 81 vs test 75. 2 days: 54 vs 68. 1 week: 42 vs 56.**
- Exp 2: SSSS vs SSST vs STTT. **5 min 83 / 78 / 71; 1 week 40 / 56 / 61.** SSSS most confident (4.8 vs 4.2 vs 4.0 on 7 points).

### Retrieval vs concept mapping - Karpicke and Blunt (2011), Science 331, 772-775 - tier A
- Exp 1: retrieval 0.67 vs concept mapping 0.45 a week later; concept mapping not significantly better than extra reading. Students predicted the reverse.
- Exp 2: 101 of 120 (84%) did better after retrieval; 90 of 120 (75%) believed concept mapping as good or better.

### Spacing - Cepeda et al. (2006) Psych Bulletin 132(3) 354-380 - tier A
- 839 assessments, 317 experiments, 184 articles. Only **12 of 271** massed-vs-spaced comparisons showed no effect or a negative one. Optimal gap grows with retention interval. Table 1: massed 36.7% vs spaced 47.3% overall.

### Spacing ridgeline - Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted, Pashler (2008) Psych Science 19(11) - tier A
- More than 1,350 people (1,354). Optimal gap **about 20-40% of a 1-week test delay, about 5-10% of a 1-year delay**. Interpolated recall optima ~3, 8, 12, 27 days for tests at 7, 35, 70, 350 days (43%, 23%, 17%, 8%). Optimal vs zero gap: 64% increase in final recall.
- CORRECTION to the brief: "10-20%" is Dunlosky's paraphrase, not the paper.

### Rereading - tier R / B
- Rawson and Kintsch (2005) J Ed Psych 97(1): massed rereading helped immediate tests, distributed rereading helped 2-day delayed tests (reported via Greving and Richter 2019 and Dunlosky 2013). Do not name the passage topic.
- Callender and McDaniel (2009) Contemp Ed Psych 34(1): "With only several exceptions, rereading did not significantly increase performance" (abstract only, tier B).

### Fluency illusion - Kornell and Bjork (2008) Psych Science 19(6) 585-592 - tier A
- Paintings by 12 artists; interleaved ("spaced") vs massed. Exp 1a .61 vs .35. **78% did better with spacing, 78% said massing was as good or better.**

### Learning styles - Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer, Bjork (2008, DOI dated 2009) PSPI 9(3) 105-119 - tier A
- Meshing hypothesis needs a crossover interaction; "virtually no evidence"; "no adequate evidence base to justify incorporating learning-styles assessments into general educational practice". Caveat: not every possible version has been tested.

### Levels of reading - Adler and Van Doren (1972), How to Read a Book - tier R
- Four cumulative levels: elementary, inspectional, analytical, syntopical. Inspectional = systematic skimming (title, preface, contents, index, blurb, pivotal chapters, last pages) + superficial reading (read a hard book straight through without stopping). Reported via Matuschak's notes and study guides: paraphrase, never quote.

### Reading a paper - Keshav (2007) ACM SIGCOMM CCR 37(3) 83-84 - tier A
- Three passes. First pass 5-10 min: title, abstract, introduction; section headings; conclusions; glance at references. Five Cs: Category, Context, Correctness, Contributions, Clarity. Second pass up to an hour, ignore proofs, study figures and axes. Third pass: virtually re-implement; 4-5 h beginner, ~1 h experienced.

### Forgetting model family - tier A / R
- Settles and Meeder (2016) ACL: p = 2^(-delta/h), half-life regression at Duolingo (tier A).
- FSRS wiki: v3 exponential 0.9^(t/S); current FSRS uses a power law that fits better (tier A).
- Ebbinghaus (1885) measured SAVINGS in relearning and fitted a log-power formula, not an exponential; "after a whole month fully one fifth" persisted (tier A, chapter 7). Never label his curve "% remembered".
- SuperMemo two-component model: stability and retrievability definitions (tier B); R = exp(-t/S) formula (tier R).

### Public-domain passages (bench) - tier A
- Franklin, Autobiography (gutenberg.org/ebooks/20203), 356 words: the Spectator imitation method.
- Darwin, The Voyage of the Beagle (ebooks/944), 454 words: Galapagos islands differ.
- Faraday, The Chemical History of a Candle (ebooks/14474), 512 words: the cup and the ascending current.
- Public domain in the US. Header removed, so the excerpts are NOT branded Project Gutenberg text; credit the book and link the page where the text was found.

## Per-session coverage

1. **What reading speed really measures** (hand-authored): Brysbaert rates, 300 myth, Rayner bottleneck, inner speech (contested, taught both ways), regressions, speed-reading tests (R), RSVP (R), skimming on purpose. Build-along: baseline with both numbers.
2. **Reading with a question**: choosing what to read and how deeply; Adler levels (R, paraphrase); inspectional = systematic skim + superficial read; questions before (purpose), during (what is the claim, what is the evidence), after (could I explain it); elaborative interrogation as a reading move (Dunlosky moderate); Rayner's effective skimming (headings, structure). Build-along: triage Mina's pile into skip / inspect / analyse with a question per item.
3. **Rereading feels like learning**: fluency illusion (Kornell and Bjork 78/78; Roediger SSSS confidence; Karpicke and Blunt predictions); Dunlosky ratings table; highlighting and rereading LOW not zero; summarization low; massed vs spaced rereading (Rawson and Kintsch, R); learning styles (Pashler, careful); turning a book into notes as retrieval: close the book, write, then check (honour PKM seam). Build-along: convert a highlighted page into five recall questions and answer them book closed.
4. **The recall bench** (hand-authored).
5. **Spacing, and the forgetting you can plan for**: Cepeda 2006 and 2008 numbers; gap depends on the deadline; Ebbinghaus savings (careful labelling); exponential vs power-law model families; elaborative interrogation and self-explanation (moderate, limited evidence); interleaving (moderate; Kornell and Bjork). Build-along: a one-month spaced plan for one reading, with prompts written in session 3.
6. **Reading a paper, a doc and a chart**: Keshav three passes and five Cs; documentation read task-first (constructed method, labelled as practice not research); chart reading from title, axes, units, baseline, source before the headline (constructed checklist; design seam to dataviz). Build-along: Mina's two papers (constructed abstracts) through the first pass, the vendor API doc task-first, and one dashboard chart axes-first.

## Not covered (honest list)
- Dyslexia and reading disorders; children learning to read; reading in a second language.
- Specific spaced-repetition apps (Anki, SuperMemo) beyond naming the model family.
- Note systems (PKM), linking (Obsidian), visual notes (Sketchnoting), chart design (Dataviz).
- Prequestion effect sizes (no source read at source; session 4 shows the learner's own split only).
- Expanding vs equal spacing: not settled by the sources read; treated as open.
- Audiobooks vs print comprehension.
