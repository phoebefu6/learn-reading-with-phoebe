## 2. Brysbaert (2019) - reading rate meta-analysis

- Citation: Brysbaert, M. (2019). How many words do we read per minute? A review and meta-analysis of reading rate. *Journal of Memory and Language*, 109, 104047. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jml.2019.104047
- URL read: https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf (author-final published PDF mirror, 30 pages, text extracted locally)
- Tier: **read-at-source: full text**

Key facts:
- 190 studies, 18,573 participants; silent adult English 238 wpm non-fiction, 260 wpm fiction. Abstract, p.1: "Based on the analysis of 190 studies (18,573 participants), we estimate that the average silent reading rate for adults in English is 238 words per minute (wpm) for non-fiction and 260 wpm for fiction." (quote over 25 words if taken whole; use the short form: "238 words per minute (wpm) for non-fiction and 260 wpm for fiction")
- Ranges (abstract, p.1): "most adults fall in the range of 175-300 wpm; for fiction the range is 200-320 wpm."
- Fiction/non-fiction gap explained by word length (abstract): "longer words in non-fiction than in fiction."
- Oral reading (abstract): "The average oral reading rate (based on 77 studies and 5965 participants) is 183 wpm."
- SD of silent non-fiction meta-analysis (p.5): "an average reading rate of 238 wpm (SD = 51.2; 95% confidence interval = 230-246). The median is 235". Weighted by N: "the mean increases to 242 wpm".
- SD of oral (p.11): "183 wpm when each study was given equal weight (SD = 25.9; 95% confidence interval = 177-189 wpm)"; weighted 180, median 181.
- Fiction figure comes from a book-reading study (p.14): "Mean reading rate was 260 wpm (SD = 87; 95% CI = 235-285)." Mean book 385 pages, 107,000 words.
- 300 wpm note (Introduction, p.1): "The normal or typical reading rate of 300 wpm is widely mentioned"; "one of the few numbers experimental psychological research has given to society."
- Brysbaert quotes Rayner et al. (2016) for college-educated adults (p.1): "about 200-400 wpm" (p. 1 of Rayner et al.); Rayner's table of 10 skilled readers averaged 308 wpm (from Rayner, 1978).
- Bottom line on 300 (Discussion, p.21): "Setting the target reading rate at 300 wpm is unrealistic for the majority of people".
- Valid population (p.21): "valid for adults between 18 and 60 years without reading problems."
- Abstract: rates "are in line with maximum listening speed and do not require the assumption of reading-specific language processing."
- Intro (p.1) summarising Rayner et al.: rates of more than 1000 wpm "are impossible without severe loss of text understanding".
- RSVP (p.18): Benedetto et al. (2015), 20+ min of an Orwell chapter via RSVP with a pause button: "participants read at a pace of 200 wpm (very similar to reading a hard copy text of the same chapter)" and did worse on comprehension than book readers.
- Skimming (p.18): rates discussed are for reading to understand the full text, "different from situations in which participants skim text for useful information." Newspaper example: 40,000 words in 1 h "reads" at 667 wpm.
- Short-burst maxima (p.18): Rubin and Turano (1992) median 790 wpm on short paragraphs; "these reading speeds are only possible for very brief time intervals".

## 1. Rayner, Schotter, Masson, Potter, Treiman (2016) - speed reading review

- Citation: Rayner, K., Schotter, E. R., Masson, M. E. J., Potter, M. C., & Treiman, R. (2016). So much to read, so little time: How do we read, and can speed reading help? *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 17(1), 4-34. https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100615623267 (PMID 26769745; not open access in PMC)
- URL read: http://faculty.cas.usf.edu/eschotter/papers/Rayner_Schotter_Masson_Potter_Treiman_2016_PSPI.pdf (second author's faculty copy of the published PDF, 31 pp., text extracted locally). Journal page numbers below.
- Tier: **read-at-source: full text**

Key facts:
- Typical rate, skilled readers (Introduction, p.4): "college-educated adults who are considered good readers usually move along at about 200 to 400 wpm." Repeated in Conclusions (p.29): "a normal pace for most readers is 200 to 400 wpm."
- Speed-accuracy trade-off (Summary, p.4): "The research shows that there is a trade-off between speed and accuracy."
- Doubling/tripling (Summary, p.4): "It is unlikely that readers will be able to double or triple their reading speeds (e.g., from around 250 to 500-750 words per minute)" while understanding as well as at normal speed.
- Skimming allowed (Summary, p.4): "speed reading or skimming the text will allow the reader to get through it faster with moderate comprehension."
- Route to faster reading (Summary, p.4): "The way to maintain high comprehension and get through text faster is to practice reading and to become a more skilled language user (e.g., through increased vocabulary)." and "language skill is at the heart of reading speed."
- Skimming rates (Introduction, p.5): "skimming rates can be as much as two to four times faster than those of typical silent reading." Comprehension lower when skimming.
- Limit is language/word identification, not eyes (Eye movements section, p.13): "language processing, rather than the ability to control the movements of one's eyes, is the primary driver of reading performance." Also: "word identification is a primary determiner of when and where readers move their eyes."
- Limit is not vision (Speed-reading courses, p.23): "the ability to read is limited by our ability to attend to, identify, and understand words rather than our ability to see them."
- Subvocalization / inner speech (p.16): suppression studies (Hardyck & Petrinovich 1970; Daneman & Newson 1992; Slowiaczek & Clifton 1980) found comprehension impaired for harder text; "inner speech is not a nuisance activity that must be eliminated, as many speed-reading proponents suggest." (p.23): "Attempts to eliminate inner speech have been shown to result in impairments in comprehension when texts are reasonably difficult". Even RSVP at 720 wpm readers "appear to generate sound-based representations of the words (Petrick, 1981)."
- Trained speed readers (p.24): Calef, Pieper & Coffey (1999): before ~280 wpm, after course ~400 wpm; comprehension fell "from pretest (81% correct) to posttest (74%)". Homa (1983): two speed readers at 15,000+ wpm, poor comprehension; ability was "a remarkable dexterity in page-turning". Liddle (1965): Wood graduates 300-1,300 wpm faster; nonfiction comprehension 68% vs 72%.
- Just, Masson & Carpenter (1980) (p.24): speed readers ~600-700 wpm, normal ~250 wpm, skimmers ~600-700 wpm; "the students of speed-reading courses are essentially being taught to skim". Speed readers' gist advantage vanished on Scientific American texts.
- Speed readers' real skill (p.25): "Effective speed readers appear to be intelligent people who already know a great deal concerning the topic".
- Effective skimming (Skimming, p.25): "Effective skimmers scan a text for headings, paragraph structure, or key words to locate potentially relevant information, then read more carefully". Readers attending to headings write the most accurate summaries (Hyona, Lorch & Kaakinen, 2002). Duggan & Payne (2009): skimming whole text beat reading half normally for important ideas.
- RSVP / Spritz (pp.26-28): Spritz removes regressions; "A regression is more likely to be the solution to discombobulation ... than a cause of it." Only two studies compared Spritz to normal reading; Benedetto et al. (2015) Orwell's 1984: 200 vs 209 wpm, more eye fatigue, poorer literal comprehension. Conclusion (p.28): RSVP apps "will not allow people to achieve the goal of greatly increasing reading speed (e.g., by doubling or tripling their normal rate) while maintaining good comprehension."
- Listening comparison (p.20): "A speaking rate of 150 to 160 wpm is comfortable"; doubling audiobook speed gives "around 300 wpm".
- Practice with language (Practice reading, p.29): "The practice that is required to become a better reader is thus practice with language". "Reading more, and reading varied texts, will increase your vocabulary and will benefit reading more than reading the same passage again and again."
- Bottom line (Conclusions, p.29): "we have seen that there is no such magic bullet. There is a trade-off between speed and accuracy in reading". Also: "it will be necessary to slow down to a normal pace in order to achieve good comprehension."
- Table 1 (p.13): 10 skilled readers (from Rayner, 1998), per-reader rates e.g. K.B. 378 wpm, J.C. 251 wpm; Brysbaert (2019) gives the table mean as 308 wpm.

## 4. Roediger and Karpicke (2006) - test-enhanced learning

- Citation: Roediger, H. L., III, & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention. *Psychological Science*, 17(3), 249-255. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x (JSTOR stable 40064526)
- URL read: https://www.hendrix.edu/uploadedFiles/Academics/Faculty_Resources/Faculty_Development_Newsletters/Testing%20effect.pdf (JSTOR scan of the published article; also mirrored at gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/2006-roediger.pdf). Text extracted locally.
- Tier: **read-at-source: full text**

Experiment 1 (pp.250-251):
- Subjects: "One hundred twenty Washington University undergraduates, ages 18 to 24".
- Materials: two TOEFL prose passages ("The Sun", "Sea Otters"), 30 idea units each, 256 and 275 words.
- Design: restudy vs test within subjects (study-study vs study-test), final-test delay 5 min / 2 days / 1 week between subjects; 7-min periods; free recall, no feedback.
- Initial test: "subjects recalled on average 20.9 idea units, or approximately 70% of the passage."
- 5 min: "restudying produced better recall than testing (81% vs. 75%)", d = 0.52.
- 2 days: "the initially tested group recalled more than the additional-study group (68% vs. 54%)", d = 0.95.
- 1 week: "The tested group recalled 56% of the material, whereas the restudy group recalled only 42%", d = 0.83.
- Crossover: tested group at 1 week recalled about as much as the restudy group at 2 days.

Experiment 2 (pp.251-252): 3 x 2 between-subjects, 30 per cell (180 total); SSSS, SSST, STTT; 5-min periods.
- 5 min: "The SSSS group recalled more than the SSST group (83% vs. 78%), who in turn recalled more than the STTT group (71%)."
- 1 week: "The STTT group recalled more than the SSST group (61% vs. 56%), who in turn recalled more than the SSSS group (40%)."
- Exposure: whole-passage readings SSSS M = 14.2, SSST 10.3, STTT 3.4.
- Predictions (Table 2, 7-point scale, "how well they thought they would remember the passage in 1 week"): SSSS 4.8, SSST 4.2, STTT 4.0. Text: "subjects in the SSSS condition were more confident that they would remember the passage in 1 week". SSSS vs SSST d = 0.54; vs STTT d = 0.61.
- Abstract: "repeated studying increased students' confidence in their ability to remember the material"; "Testing is a powerful means of improving learning, not just assessing it."

## 5a. Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted, Rohrer (2006) - distributed practice meta-analysis

- Citation: Cepeda, N. J., Pashler, H., Vul, E., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D. (2006). Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: A review and quantitative synthesis. *Psychological Bulletin*, 132(3), 354-380. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354 (PMID 16719566)
- URL read: https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaPashlerVulWixtedRohrer-PB-2006.pdf (co-author Vul's lab copy of the published article, 27 pp.). Text extracted locally.
- Tier: **read-at-source: full text**

Key facts:
- Scope (abstract, p.354): "This review found 839 assessments of distributed practice in 317 experiments located in 184 articles."
- Lag x retention interval (abstract): "the ISI producing maximal retention increased as retention interval increased."
- Spacing vs massed (pp.358-359): 271 comparisons (23 effect sizes). "Spaced presentations led to markedly better final-test performance, compared with massed presentations." Retention <1 min: "improved final-test performance by 9%".
- Robustness (p.359): "Only 12 of 271 comparisons of massed and spaced performance showed no effect or a negative effect from spacing, making the spacing effect quite robust."
- Table 1 (p.359), percent correct massed vs spaced, all retention intervals: 36.7% vs 47.3% (271 differences, 254 studies, 14,811 participants). By interval: 1-59 s 41.2 vs 50.1; 1 day 32.9 vs 43.0; 2-7 days 31.1 vs 45.4; 8-30 days 32.8 vs 62.2; 31+ days 17.0 vs 39.0 (one study).
- Spacing benefit not modulated by retention interval (General Discussion, p.365): "for the spacing effect proper, we failed to find any evidence that the effect is modulated by retention interval."
- Lag summary (p.362): literature shows "nonmonotonic effect of absolute ISI upon memory performance" and "the positive relationship between retention interval and the optimal absolute ISI".
- Summary (p.371): "Distributing learning across different days (instead of grouping learning episodes within a single day) greatly improves the amount of material retained".
- Sibling PKM course already quotes the "12 of 271" and "quite robust" lines correctly.

## 5b. Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted, Pashler (2008) - temporal ridgeline

- Citation: Cepeda, N. J., Vul, E., Rohrer, D., Wixted, J. T., & Pashler, H. (2008). Spacing effects in learning: A temporal ridgeline of optimal retention. *Psychological Science*, 19(11), 1095-1102. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x (PMID 19076480)
- URL read: https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf (published PDF, 8 pp.)
- Tier: **read-at-source: full text**

**CORRECTION to the brief:** the brief said "10-20% at 1 week". The paper says 20-40% at 1 week, falling to 5-10% at 1 year. The "15 to 20%" figure is a different statement (an extrapolation from an earlier benchmark study, p.1096).

Key facts:
- Abstract (p.1095): "more than 1,350 individuals were taught a set of facts and - after a gap of up to 3.5 months - given a review." Final test "at a further delay of up to 1 year."
- Abstract: "the optimal gap declined from about 20 to 40% of a 1-week test delay to about 5 to 10% of a 1-year test delay."
- Abstract: "The interaction of gap and test delay implies that many educational practices are highly inefficient."
- N (p.1096): "1,354 new subjects from our laboratory's Internet Memory Research panel".
- Earlier benchmark extrapolation (p.1096): "about a 15 to 20% ratio of gap to RI optimizes retention, but linearity cannot be assumed."
- Results (p.1099): optimal vs zero-day gap "provided a 64% increase in final recall, d = 1.1".
- Optimal gaps tested, RIs 7/35/70/350 days: recall 1, 11, 21, 21 days. Interpolated recall optima "approximately 3, 8, 12, and 27 days (corresponding to 43%, 23%, 17%, and 8% of the RIs". Recognition: 1.6, 7, 10, 25 days (24%, 19%, 14%, 7%).
- Recall gain at optimal gap vs zero gap: "10, 59, 111, and 77% improvement in recall" for RIs 7, 35, 70, 350 days.
- Fitted surface example (p.1099): "the optimal gap for the 350-day RI is 23 days, which is just 7% of the RI."
- Shape (abstract): "an increase in the interstudy gap at first increased, and then gradually reduced, final test performance."

## 11. Keshav (2007) - How to read a paper (three-pass method)

- Citation: Keshav, S. (2007). How to read a paper. *ACM SIGCOMM Computer Communication Review*, 37(3), 83-84. https://doi.org/10.1145/1273445.1273458
- URL read: http://ccr.sigcomm.org/online/files/p83-keshavA.pdf (published 2-page PDF)
- Tier: **read-at-source: full text**

Key facts:
- Core idea (s.2, p.83): "you should read the paper in up to three passes, instead of starting at the beginning and plowing your way to the end."
- Pass purposes (s.2): "The first pass gives you a general idea about the paper. The second pass lets you grasp the paper's content, but not its details. The third pass helps you understand the paper in depth."
- First pass (s.2.1): "about five to ten minutes". Steps: (1) carefully read title, abstract, introduction; (2) read section and sub-section headings, "but ignore everything else"; (3) read the conclusions; (4) glance over the references.
- The five Cs (s.2.1): Category ("What type of paper is this?"), Context ("Which other papers is it related to?"), Correctness ("Do the assumptions appear to be valid?"), Contributions ("What are the paper's main contributions?"), Clarity ("Is the paper well written?").
- First pass can end reading (s.2.1): "Using this information, you may choose not to read further."
- Second pass (s.2.2): "read the paper with greater care, but ignore details such as proofs." Look carefully at figures, graphs, axes, error bars; mark unread references. Duration (p.84): "The second pass should take up to an hour." Outcome: "summarize the main thrust of the paper, with supporting evidence, to someone else."
- Third pass (s.2.3, p.84): "The key to the third pass is to attempt to virtually re-implement the paper". Duration: "about four or five hours for beginners, and about an hour for an experienced reader." Outcome: "reconstruct the entire structure of the paper from memory".
- Writers' corollary (s.2.1): "you can expect most reviewers (and readers) to make only one pass over it."
- Note: the paper's DOI given here is the commonly cited ACM DOI; the PDF itself does not print it.

## 8. Forgetting-curve model family (Ebbinghaus; SuperMemo two-component; FSRS; Settles and Meeder HLR)

### 8a. Settles and Meeder (2016) - half-life regression
- Citation: Settles, B., & Meeder, B. (2016). A trainable spaced repetition model for language learning. In *Proceedings of the 54th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics* (Vol. 1: Long Papers, pp. 1848-1858). Berlin: ACL. https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/P16-1174
- URL read: https://aclanthology.org/P16-1174.pdf
- Tier: **read-at-source: full text**
- Model (s.3.3, p.1851): Ebbinghaus model "posits that memory decays exponentially over time: p = 2^(-Delta/h)." p = probability of recall, Delta = "the lag time since the item was last practiced", h = "the half-life or measure of strength in the learner's long-term memory."
- Cases (s.3.3): Delta = 0 gives p = 1.0; Delta = h gives "p = 2^-1 = 0.5, and the student is on the verge of being unable to remember."
- Half-life estimate (s.3.3, eq.2): h_hat = 2^(Theta . x), "Under the assumption that half-life should increase exponentially with each repeated exposure". Pimsleur and Leitner are "special cases of (2) using a few fixed, hand-picked weights."
- Prediction resets after each exposure: p_hat "resets to 1.0 after each exposure, since Delta = 0".
- Results (abstract): "reducing error by 45%+ compared to several baselines at predicting student recall rates"; "improve Duolingo daily student engagement by 12% in an operational user study." Data: 12.9 million student-word lesson instances (s.4.1, p.1853). Follow-up user experiment with 3.3 million students over two weeks (p.1855).
- Intro claim about Ebbinghaus (s.1): comparable results "with half as many practices spread out over three days." (Settles' paraphrase of Ebbinghaus, not read in Ebbinghaus here.)

### 8b. Ebbinghaus (1885) - original savings curve
- Citation: Ebbinghaus, H. (1885/1913). *Memory: A Contribution to Experimental Psychology* (H. A. Ruger & C. E. Bussenius, Trans.). New York: Teachers College, Columbia University. (Original: *Uber das Gedachtnis*, 1885.)
- URL read: https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Ebbinghaus/memory7.htm (Chapter 7, Classics in the History of Psychology, York University)
- Tier: **read-at-source: full text of chapter (tables are images, not read)**
- Section 29: "After 24 hours about one third was always remembered; after 6 days about one fourth, and after a whole month fully one fifth of the first work persisted in effect." Also cites "33.7 per cent for the relearning after 24 hours".
- Section 30 refers back to "58 per cent" savings after 19 minutes (13-syllable series).
- Fitted formula (s.29): b = 100k / ((log t)^c + k), b = saving in relearning, t in minutes, "k = 1.84 and c = 1.25" (common logs). NOTE: Ebbinghaus's own fit is a log-power form, NOT an exponential; the exponential form is a later modelling convention. The commonly quoted table (58.2, 44.2, 35.8, 33.7, 27.8, 25.4, 21.1) is in table images in s.28 that I could not read - those exact values are **reported** (secondary, widely reproduced), except 33.7 which is in the text.
- Savings is a relearning measure (time saved relearning), not percent recalled - do not label the curve "% remembered" without saying so.

### 8d. FSRS (open-spaced-repetition)
- URL read: https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/awesome-fsrs/wiki/The-Algorithm
- Tier: **read-at-source: full text (wiki)**
- Stability definition: "Stability (interval when R=90%)".
- FSRS v1/v3: R(t,S) = 0.9^(t/S) (exponential). FSRS v4: R(t,S) = (1 + t/(9S))^-1. FSRS-4.5/5: R(t,S) = (1 + FACTOR * t/S)^DECAY, DECAY = -0.5, FACTOR = 19/81. FSRS-6: R = (1 + factor * t/S)^(-w20), factor = 0.9^(-1/w20) - 1 (power law).
- Use for the simulator: the exponential family R = 2^(-t/h) (HLR) or R = 0.9^(t/S) (FSRS v3) is a defensible ILLUSTRATIVE model; current FSRS uses a power law because it fits real data better. Label any simulator curve as a model, not data.

## 6. Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer, Bjork (2008) - learning styles

- Citation: Pashler, H., McDaniel, M., Rohrer, D., & Bjork, R. (2008). Learning styles: Concepts and evidence. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 9(3), 105-119. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x (issue dated 2008; copyright line and DOI say 2009 - cite as 2008 with the DOI, or note "2008/2009")
- URL read: https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Pashler_McDaniel_Rohrer_Bjork_2009_PSPI.pdf (Bjork lab copy of published PDF, 15 pp.)
- Tier: **read-at-source: full text**

Key facts:
- Meshing hypothesis (Summary, p.105): "the meshing hypothesis, according to which instruction is best provided in a format that matches the preferences of the learner".
- Required design (Summary, p.105, and p.109): learners divided into groups by learning style; within each group "randomly assigned to one of at least two different learning methods"; "all subjects must be given the same test of achievement"; the best method for one group must differ from the best for the other.
- Crossover (p.109): support "if and only if an experiment reveals what is commonly known as a crossover interaction between learning style and method".
- Finding (Summary, p.105): "we found virtually no evidence for the interaction pattern mentioned above". Studies with an appropriate method "found results that flatly contradict the popular meshing hypothesis."
- Conclusion (Summary, p.105): "at present, there is no adequate evidence base to justify incorporating learning-styles assessments into general educational practice."
- Resources (p.105): "limited education resources would better be devoted to adopting other educational practices that have a strong evidence base".
- Closing (Summary, p.117): "The contrast between the enormous popularity of the learning-styles approach within education and the lack of credible evidence for its utility is, in our opinion, striking and disturbing."
- Caveat they state (p.105): it "would be an error to conclude that all possible versions of learning styles have been tested".

## 9. Karpicke and Blunt (2011) - retrieval practice vs concept mapping

- Citation: Karpicke, J. D., & Blunt, J. R. (2011). Retrieval practice produces more learning than elaborative studying with concept mapping. *Science*, 331(6018), 772-775. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1199327
- URL read: https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2011/2011_Karpicke_Blunt_Science.pdf (Karpicke lab copy of the published article)
- Tier: **read-at-source: full text**

Key facts:
- Abstract (p.772): "practicing retrieval produces greater gains in meaningful learning than elaborative studying with concept mapping." Advantage held on inference questions and "even when the criterial test involved creating concept maps."
- Exp 1 design (p.772): "Eighty undergraduate students"; four conditions: study once, repeated study (four consecutive study periods), elaborative concept mapping, retrieval practice (study then free recall, restudy, recall again). Science texts; final test 1 week later.
- Exp 1 results (p.773): retrieval practice "produced the best learning, better than elaborative studying with concept mapping, which itself was not significantly better than spending additional time reading." Retrieval M = 0.67 vs concept mapping M = 0.45, "about a 50% improvement in long-term retention scores", d = 1.50.
- Metacognition Exp 1 (p.773): "Students predicted that repeated studying would produce the best long-term retention and that practicing retrieval would produce the worst retention, even though the opposite was true".
- Exp 2 (p.773-774): 120 students, within-subjects; retrieval vs concept mapping d = 1.07 on short-answer; on a final concept-mapping test retrieval still better, d = 1.01.
- Individuals (p.774): "101 out of 120 students (84%) performed better on the final test after practicing retrieval". And "Ninety out of 120 students (75%) believed that elaborative concept mapping would be just as effective or even more effective".

## 10. Rereading: Rawson and Kintsch (2005); Callender and McDaniel (2009)

### 10a. Rawson and Kintsch (2005)
- Citation: Rawson, K. A., & Kintsch, W. (2005). Rereading effects depend on time of test. *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 97(1), 70-80. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.97.1.70 (Crossref confirmed)
- URL read: none at source (APA, paywalled; no author PDF found; Rawson lab page lists it without a PDF). Abstract elided by publisher in Semantic Scholar.
- Tier: **reported: secondary sources** - (i) Greving, C. E., & Richter, T. (2019). Distributed learning in the classroom: Effects of rereading schedules depend on time of test. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 9, 2517. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02517, read at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6333692/ (full text); (ii) Dunlosky et al. (2013), s.7 and s.9.2d (full text, see source 3).
- As reported by Greving & Richter (Exp 1): text read "once or twice"; second reading immediately (massed) or 1 week later (distributed). "Recall and text comprehension performance were measured either immediately after reading or after a delay of 2 days."
- Immediate test (G&R): massed rereaders "outperformed students in the single reading condition"; "no differences were found between the distributed reading and single reading conditions." In recall, massed even beat distributed.
- Delayed 2-day test (G&R): "Students in the distributed condition outperformed those in the massed and single reading condition". "Thus, the benefits of distributed rereading depended on time of test."
- Dunlosky et al. 2013 (p.38): "massed practice (cramming) actually benefitting performance on immediate tests (e.g., Rawson & Kintsch, 2005)." Dunlosky (p.28): massed-rereading effects failed "after 1-2 days" in Rawson & Kintsch (2005) among others.
- DISCREPANCY to flag: Greving & Richter describe the text as "an expository text about carbon sequestration (1730 words)"; Dunlosky et al. (p.28) say Rawson (2012), Rawson & Kintsch (2005) and Verkoeijen et al. (2008) "used the same text" about the charge of the Light Brigade. Possibly different experiments used different texts. Do not name the passage topic on the page without reading the original.

### 10b. Callender and McDaniel (2009)
- Citation: Callender, A. A., & McDaniel, M. A. (2009). The limited benefits of rereading educational texts. *Contemporary Educational Psychology*, 34(1), 30-41. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2008.07.001 (Crossref confirmed)
- URL read: https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/the-limited-benefits-of-rereading-educational-texts/ (WashU research profile carrying the abstract). ScienceDirect returned 403.
- Tier: **read-at-source: abstract only**
- Abstract (as relayed by the fetch tool, which limited quoting): four experiments; rereading vs single reading of textbook chapters or a Scientific American article; tested by multiple-choice, short-answer and summaries. Verbatim: "With only several exceptions, rereading did not significantly increase performance on the assessments." Reading comprehension ability did not moderate it; immediate rereading may offer little benefit for such tests.
- Dunlosky et al. (2013, p.28) corroborate: Callender & McDaniel "did not find an effect of massed rereading over single reading for either higher- or lower-ability readers"; also no effects on inference questions and none after 1-2 days.
- Dunlosky summary of the rereading literature (p.28-29): "spaced rereading consistently outperforms massed rereading on delayed tests." And (p.29) "no experimental research has assessed its impact on learning in educational contexts."

### Note on the Cepeda (2008) ratio
Dunlosky et al. (2013, p.38) paraphrase Cepeda et al. (2008) as "the ideal lag between practice sessions would be approximately 10-20% of the desired retention interval." That is the likely origin of the "10-20%" in the brief. The 2008 paper's own abstract says 20-40% at 1 week falling to 5-10% at 1 year (see 5b). Cite the primary source's numbers.

## 12. Illusion of fluency - Kornell and Bjork (2008)

- Citation: Kornell, N., & Bjork, R. A. (2008). Learning concepts and categories: Is spacing the "enemy of induction"? *Psychological Science*, 19(6), 585-592. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02127.x
- URL read: https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Kornell_Bjork_2008_PsychScience.pdf (Bjork lab copy of published PDF, 8 pp.)
- Tier: **read-at-source: full text**
- (Koriat & Bjork 2005 not read - not needed; Kornell & Bjork carries the number. Roediger & Karpicke 2006 and Karpicke & Blunt 2011 above also carry confidence/prediction reversals.)

Key facts:
- Design (p.586-587): UCLA undergraduates, 120 in Exp 1a, 72 in Exp 1b (36 per condition). Paintings by 12 artists (10 each); a given artist's paintings shown consecutively (massed) or interleaved with other artists' (spaced). Test: name the artist of new paintings.
- Abstract (p.585): "Surprisingly, induction profited from spacing, even though massing apparently created a sense of fluent learning". "Participants rated massing as more effective than spacing, even after their own test performance had demonstrated the opposite."
- First-test-block accuracy (p.588): Exp 1a spaced M = .61 vs massed M = .35, d = 0.99; Exp 1b spaced .59 vs massed .36, d = 1.28.
- The headline mismatch (p.588): "78% of the participants did better with spaced presentations than they did with massed presentations, but 78% of the participants said that massing was as good as or better than spacing."
- Note the terminology: Kornell & Bjork call the interleaved condition "spaced"; in Dunlosky's terms this is closer to interleaving.

## 7. Adler and Van Doren (1972) - How to Read a Book

- Citation: Adler, M. J., & Van Doren, C. (1972). *How to Read a Book: The Classic Guide to Intelligent Reading* (Rev. and updated ed.). New York: Simon & Schuster (Touchstone). (First edition by Adler alone, 1940.) ISBN 9780671212094.
- URL read: https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zFSbY4oPcbeKpotrJVf1A8P (Andy Matuschak's reading notes on the 1972 2nd ed., no page numbers); corroborated by https://www.supersummary.com/how-to-read-a-book/summary and https://www.shortform.com/blog/four-levels-of-reading (study guides). Publisher page (simonandschuster.com) returned 403. The book itself is in copyright and was not read.
- Tier: **reported: secondary sources (Matuschak notes; SuperSummary; Shortform)**

Key facts (all reported, no page numbers):
- Four levels: elementary, inspectional, analytical, syntopical. They are called "levels" (not "kinds") because they are cumulative - each includes the ones below (Matuschak; SuperSummary).
- Elementary: basic literacy, what the words on the page say.
- Inspectional = the best reading possible "given a limited time" (Matuschak, quoting the book). Two sub-levels:
  - Systematic skimming (pre-reading): title page and preface; table of contents; index; publisher's blurb; pivotal chapters, especially opening/closing statements; thumb through reading scattered paragraphs and the last two or three pages. Reader as a "detective looking for clues to a book's general theme or idea" (as quoted by Matuschak).
  - Superficial reading: read a difficult book straight through first; rule as quoted by Matuschak: "Read it through without stopping to look up or ponder things you don't understand."
- Analytical: the best reading given unlimited time; aims at understanding, not just information (Part Two covers classifying the book, x-raying it, coming to terms with the author, criticizing fairly - SuperSummary).
- Syntopical: reading several books on one subject together, framing the issues and ranging the authors' opposing answers (SuperSummary/Shortform).
- Safe phrasing for the page: name the four levels and the two-part inspectional reading; avoid quoting the book's own sentences as verbatim (we only have them second-hand).
