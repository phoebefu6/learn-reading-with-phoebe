Spacing, and the forgetting you can plan for

Forgetting is not a personal failing, it is a curve, and a curve can be scheduled against. Tonight we read what Ebbinghaus actually measured in 1885 (it was not "percent remembered"), why modern models disagree about the shape of the tail, and what two large reviews of spacing found: the gap between reviews matters, and the best gap depends on when you need the material. Then we make each review count for more with three moves rated moderate by the evidence, and put a month of reviews for one reading on one calendar.

Part 1 · covers what forgetting looks like

The curve everyone draws, and what was actually measured

5 min live

The forgetting curve is usually drawn as "percent remembered" falling over days, credited to Hermann Ebbinghaus. His 1885 measure was something else: how much time he saved when he relearned material he had learned before. And the shape of the fall is still argued about, because the shape decides how much is left in the long tail, which is exactly where a month-long deadline sits.

LiveEbbinghaus measured savings, not memories3 min▶

Ebbinghaus learned series by heart, waited, then learned the same series again and timed it. His measure was savings in relearning: how much less time the second learning took than the first. A series that felt completely gone could still be relearned faster, so savings picks up memory that a "can you recall it?" test would score as zero.

His chapter 7 is careful about the tail. After a whole month, fully one fifth of the work of the first learning was still saved. And the formula he fitted to his numbers was logarithmic and power-like, not the clean exponential later drawings suggest. So the honest one-line version is: forgetting is fast at first and slows down, and what is left after a month is smaller but measurable. Any curve labelled "percent remembered" is making a claim about recall that he did not measure.

LiveTwo model families, and why the session 4 model picked one3 min▶

Modern scheduling models write forgetting as a formula. Settles and Meeder's half-life regression (ACL 2016), used at Duolingo, writes recall as p = 2^(-delta/h): delta is the time since you last practised, h is the item's half-life, and every half-life that passes halves the chance of recall. That is the exponential family. An earlier version of the FSRS scheduler used the same family, as 0.9^(t/S).

The current FSRS uses a power law instead, because it fits real review data better: a power law falls fast early and keeps a longer tail. The session 4 model uses the exponential on purpose, because it is simple enough to compute and explain on one page, and it says so. For a month-long horizon the difference matters: an exponential is pessimistic about the tail.

Self-studyStability and retrievability: two numbers, not one3 min▶

SuperMemo's two-component model separates two things that "how well do I know this?" lumps together. Retrievability is how likely you are to recall the item right now. Stability is how slowly that likelihood falls, in effect how long the memory lasts. Straight after a review, retrievability is high for every item; stability is what differs, and it is what a good review raises.

The model is commonly reported with the formula R = exp(-t/S), the same one the session 4 model uses. The definitions are read at source; the formula is reported. The practical reading: a review that feels easy because you did it ten minutes ago tells you about retrievability, and almost nothing about stability. That is why the gap matters, and it is Part 2.

Real world

A forgetting curve in production. Settles and Meeder built half-life regression for Duolingo, where it estimates a half-life for each word a learner practises and uses it to decide what to show next. The model is a working answer to the question this session asks of your own reading: given what I know about how fast this fades, when should I see it again?

Part 2 · covers the spacing evidence and the gap that follows the deadline

Spacing works, and the best gap grows with the deadline

5 min live

The spacing effect is well replicated, and two papers by Nicholas Cepeda and colleagues give it numbers: a 2006 review of the whole literature on verbal recall, and a 2008 experiment with more than 1,350 people that mapped how the best gap between two study sessions changes with the time until the test.

LiveCepeda 2006: the whole literature in one count3 min▶

Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted and Rohrer (Psychological Bulletin, 2006) pooled 839 assessments of distributed practice from 317 experiments in 184 articles on verbal recall. When they compared spreading study out against massing it together, only 12 of 271 comparisons showed no effect or a negative one. The rest favoured spacing.

The review's second finding is the one that becomes a plan: the best gap is not a fixed number. It grows as the time until the test grows. A gap that is ideal for a test next week is too short for a test next year.

LiveCepeda 2008: the ridgeline3 min▶

Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted and Pashler (Psychological Science, 2008) taught more than 1,350 people a set of facts, reviewed them after one of many gaps, and tested them after one of four delays: 7, 35, 70 or 350 days. Plotting recall against gap and delay gives a ridge, and the top of the ridge moves. The interpolated best gaps were about 3, 8, 12 and 27 days for the four tests.

As shares of the wait, those are 43, 23, 17 and 8 percent. The paper's own summary is that the optimal gap is about 20 to 40 percent of a one-week test delay and about 5 to 10 percent of a one-year delay. And it was worth getting right: the optimal gap against no gap at all gave a 64 percent increase in final recall. A rule of thumb you can use tonight: for a test about a month away, a first gap of about a week is near the 35-day optimum. It comes from one study of facts, so treat it as a starting point, not a law.

Self-studyExpanding or equal gaps: not settled here3 min▶

A common recommendation is that gaps should expand: a day, then three, then a week. The sources read for these pages do not settle whether expanding gaps beat equal ones, so treat it as an open question and do not let anyone sell it to you as proven.

The session 4 model seems to have an opinion, and it is worth seeing why it is not evidence. The session 4 model prints that the day 1/3/7 schedule leads at day 14 (78.5 against 67.5 percent) but the day 1/7/21 schedule leads at day 30 (72.8 against 46.5 percent). That ordering comes from settings chosen by hand, fitted to nobody. What it does illustrate, and what Cepeda's human data agree with, is that the better schedule depends on when the test is.

Real world

Mina's two deadlines (constructed). Mina has four days to Friday's decision and a month until the contract review, where she must still explain why. A review tomorrow serves Friday; it does little for the month. Cepeda's ridge says the review that protects the month should come about a week after her reading, and the build-along below puts it in her calendar.

Part 3 · covers making each review count: elaboration, self-explanation, interleaving

Five minutes of review, spent well

5 min live

Spacing decides when you review. What you do inside the five minutes decides how much each review is worth. Dunlosky and colleagues (Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2013) rated ten learning techniques. Two came out high: practice testing and distributed practice, which are retrieval and spacing. Three came out moderate, and all three fit inside a review.

LiveElaboration and self-explanation: moderate, and why3 min▶

Elaborative interrogation means asking "why is this true?" about a fact and answering it. Self-explanation means explaining how one step follows from the last, or how a new piece connects to what you know. Dunlosky and colleagues rated both moderate. Not because they failed, but because the evidence for them is limited, so how far they reach across materials, learners and delays is less established than for practice testing and spacing.

As review prompts they cost seconds. After answering a recall prompt from memory and checking it, add one of two questions: "why is this true?" for a fact, and "how does this step follow from the last?" for an argument or a procedure. If you cannot answer, you have found the part you only recognised.

LiveInterleaving: mix the topics inside a review3 min▶

Interleaving means mixing topics or kinds of problem within a session instead of finishing one before starting the next. Dunlosky and colleagues rate it moderate. The cleanest case is Kornell and Bjork (Psychological Science, 2008): people learned the styles of 12 painters from examples shown either grouped by artist or mixed together. In experiment 1a, the mixed condition scored .61 against .35 for the grouped one.

Then the uncomfortable part: 78 percent of participants did better with the mixed schedule, and 78 percent said grouping had been as good or better. Mixing feels harder and less fluent, which is the fluency illusion from session 3 again. In a review, it means your next card comes from a different topic, even though staying on one feels smoother.

Self-studyThe two rated high, and the plan that combines them3 min▶

Of the ten techniques, only practice testing and distributed practice were rated high utility. Dunlosky and colleagues also note that spaced rereading consistently beats massed rereading on delayed tests, so even the low-rated move improves when it is spread out. Rereading and highlighting were rated low utility, not useless: they do not consistently boost performance.

The plan tonight combines the two high ones and adds the moderate ones for free: retrieval (practice testing) on a spaced calendar (distributed practice), with one why question (elaboration) and mixed topics (interleaving) inside each session. Where those prompts live long term is a note-system question, and the PKM sessions cover it at learn-pkm-with-phoebe.

Real world

The 78 and the 78. In Kornell and Bjork's painting study, the same share of people who learned better from the mixed schedule also judged it no better than grouping. When a review feels clumsy because the topics keep switching, that feeling is not a measure of how much is going in.

Build-along

A month on one calendar

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Pick one reading you must still know in about a month: a report you will be asked about, a policy you will apply, a paper you will cite. You will write the prompts, set the first gap from the deadline, and put three five-minute reviews in your real calendar before the session ends. Mina does it alongside you with the vendor report (constructed); use your own material.

Pick one reading and name its deadline. One reading, not your whole pile. Write down the date you must still know it, and what "knowing it" means on that date: explaining it, applying it, defending a decision.

★ Step 1 · Mina's reading and deadlineReading: vendor report, pages 1-12 (pricing and integration) Read on: Mon 1 Deadline: Wed 31, contract review: explain why Vendor A Horizon: 30 days

Write five to eight recall prompts. Reuse the questions you wrote from your highlighted page in session 3; add a few until you have five to eight. Each one must be answerable from memory in a sentence, and its answer must be checkable against the text.

★ Step 2 · Mina's prompts (from session 3, plus two)1) How does each vendor price? 2) At what monthly pick volume does A stop being cheaper? 3) What extra layer does B's integration need? 4) How long is A's contract? 5) How long is the pilot? 6) What SLA does the report quote? 7) Which risk did the report rate highest for A?

Set the first gap from the deadline, and show the arithmetic. Cepeda's ridge: about 20 to 40 percent of the wait for a one-week test, about 5 to 10 percent for a one-year test, and an optimum of about 8 days (23 percent) for a 35-day test. Pick the published horizon nearest yours and use its share. This is a rule of thumb from one study of facts, not a law.

★ Step 3 · the first gapHorizon: 30 days -> nearest published test: 35 days, best gap ~8 days (23%) 23% x 30 days = 6.9 -> first gap: 7 days Rule of thumb from Cepeda et al. 2008; your horizon and share will differ.

Put three dated sessions in your calendar now. Five minutes each, with a reminder. Whether later gaps should stay equal or expand is not settled by the evidence read here; equal gaps are a fine default. Make sure the last review lands before the deadline, not after it: the session 4 model showed what happens when it does not.

★ Step 4 · Mina's calendar entriesMon 8 09:00 Review 1 · vendor report · 5 min Mon 15 09:00 Review 2 · vendor report · 5 min Mon 22 09:00 Review 3 · vendor report · 5 min Wed 31 Contract review (the test)

Add one elaboration prompt per session. After the recall prompts, one "why is this true?" or "how does this follow?" question. Mix the order of the prompts each time so neighbouring cards are on different points, which is interleaving on a small scale.

★ Step 5 · one why question per reviewReview 1: Why does per-pick pricing favour A below the break-even volume? Review 2: How does B's middleware need follow from its API design? Review 3: Why would a 3-year contract change the risk of choosing A? Order: shuffle prompts 1-7 each time

Log predicted against actual, every time. Before each review, write how many prompts you think you will answer correctly. Then answer them from memory, check, and write the real count. The gap between the two numbers is your own measure of the fluency illusion.

If a review goes badly, keep the date. A miss on a prompt is not a failed review; retrieving after some forgetting is what spacing is for. Check the answer, ask the why question, and keep the next session where it is rather than cramming an extra one tonight.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A slide shows "the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve: percent remembered after each day". What did Ebbinghaus actually measure?

Ebbinghaus measured savings in relearning, and after a whole month fully one fifth of the original work was still saved. He fitted a log-power formula, not an exponential, to his own data. "Percent remembered" is a later relabelling.

2 · According to Cepeda and colleagues (2008), how does the best gap between two study sessions change as the test moves further away?

The interpolated optima were about 3, 8, 12 and 27 days for tests at 7, 35, 70 and 350 days: 43, 23, 17 and 8 percent of the wait. Longer horizons want longer gaps in days and smaller shares.

3 · Why did Dunlosky and colleagues rate elaborative interrogation and self-explanation moderate rather than high?

Moderate reflects the limits of the evidence, not a failure in it. Learning styles have no adequate evidence base for the matching version, and a why question takes seconds, so it fits in any review.
