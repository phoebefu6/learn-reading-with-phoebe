Reading with a question

Part 1 · covers choosing what to read and how deeply

Not every item deserves the same depth

5 min live

Most people have one reading mode: open the document at page one and keep going until time runs out. Mortimer Adler and Charles Van Doren's How to Read a Book (1972 revised edition), as reported in secondary notes and study guides, describes reading as a set of levels you choose between. The useful move for a working pile is the decision before you start: what do I need from this item, and so which level does it get?

LiveFour levels of reading, each containing the last2 min▶

As reported in Andy Matuschak's reading notes and two study guides (the book itself is in copyright and was not read for these pages), Adler and Van Doren name four levels. Elementary reading is knowing what the words on the page say. Inspectional reading is getting the most out of a text in a limited time. Analytical reading is the best you can do with unlimited time, aimed at understanding rather than information. Syntopical reading puts several texts on one subject side by side and sets out where they disagree.

They call them levels rather than kinds because they are cumulative: each one includes the ones below it. You cannot read a book analytically without also, at some point, having inspected it. That ordering is the practical point. The inspectional level is not a lesser substitute for real reading; it is the first step of real reading, and for many items in a working pile it is also the last one they need.

LiveInspectional reading is a skim with a method2 min▶

The reported description has two parts. The first is systematic skimming: read the title and the preface, then the table of contents, then the index, then the publisher's blurb; look at the chapters that seem pivotal, especially their opening and closing statements; thumb through, reading a paragraph here and there, and read the last few pages. The question you are answering is narrow: what is this text about, how is it built, and does it hold what I need?

The second is superficial reading of a hard book: go straight through once without stopping to look things up or puzzle over what you do not understand, so that you have the whole shape before you go back for the difficult parts. Neither part is "speed reading". Session 1 showed that speed reading trades understanding for pace at every point on the curve. Inspectional reading is honest about what it gets: a map, not the territory.

For a work document the same moves translate directly: contents page, executive summary, section headings, the figures, the recommendations or conclusion, the last page. For a research paper, session 6 uses a published three-pass method that starts the same way.

Self-studyThe skip decision is a successful read3 min▶

A first look that ends with "not now" feels like failure, because you opened something and did not finish it. It is the opposite. You spent a minute or two and bought a decision: this item does not answer any question you have this week. The cost of not skipping is the hour you would have spent reading it whole because you started it.

Two habits keep skips honest. Write the one-line reason ("no pricing data", "covers B2C, we are B2B", "superseded by the 2025 version") so that future you does not open it again for the same reason. And phrase it as "not now", not "never": a skip is tied to this week's question, and next month's question may need the item. Where those one-liners live long term is a note system, which is the next-door Learn PKM with Phoebe course.

Real world

Mina triages her pile (constructed). Mina writes one line next to each item. The second paper on picking errors turns out, from its abstract, to study manual warehouses with no software at all: skip, two minutes, reason written. The vendor report gets an inspect, not a cover-to-cover read, because her question is about pricing and the contents page shows pricing is one chapter. Only the order dashboard gets an analytical read tonight, because her real question, "which vendor is cheaper for us?", cannot be answered without last year's pick volume. No number about Mina is a measurement.

Part 2 · covers questions before, during and after

A question tells you where to slow down

5 min live

Session 1 described what effective skimmers do, as summarised by Rayner and colleagues (2016): scan headings, paragraph structure and key words to find what might be relevant, then read those parts more carefully. The step that review leaves implicit is how a reader knows what counts as relevant. The answer is a question, written down before the text is opened, that the text has to answer for you.

LiveBefore: write the question the text must answer3 min▶

Write one question per item, before you open it, in words specific enough that you would recognise the answer when you saw it. "Learn about the vendors" is not a question; it has no answer. "At our volume, which vendor costs less per month?" has one. A good before-question for a decision names the decision, names what would settle it, and is small enough that one item could answer it.

This is the purpose that makes skimming work. Rayner and colleagues describe effective skimmers as people who scan headings, structure and key words, then slow down where the text is relevant. Two studies they summarise point the same way, as reported in the review: readers who paid attention to headings wrote the most accurate summaries (Hyönä, Lorch and Kaakinen, 2002), and skimming a whole text beat reading half of it normally for picking up its important ideas (Duggan and Payne, 2009). A question is what tells you which heading matters.

LiveDuring: claim, evidence, and what would change my mind2 min▶

Once you are reading slowly, hold three questions over every paragraph that matters:

  • What is the claim? One sentence, in your words. If you cannot write it, you have not found it yet, and that is the signal to reread the paragraph rather than move on.
  • What is the evidence? A measurement, a case, a citation, an argument, or nothing. "Customers report big savings" is a claim with a marketing team for evidence.
  • What would change my mind? The fact that, if true, would flip your decision. For Mina: if Harbourline's pick volume were well above the break-even, the cheaper vendor would swap.

These three are a reading practice, not a research finding; they are offered as a method and no effect size is attached to them.

Self-studyAfter: could I explain it without the text?3 min▶

When you finish, close the document and try to answer your before-question out loud or on paper, to someone who has not read it. If you can, you have the answer. If you find yourself reaching for the page, you had the feeling of understanding without the understanding itself.

That check is not just a test of the reading. Pulling an answer out of memory is retrieval, and practice testing, which works by retrieval, is one of the two techniques Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) rated high utility. Session 3 shows why rereading feels like learning when it is not, and session 4 lets you measure the gap on yourself. For now, the habit is enough: the read is not finished until the book is closed and the answer is written.

Real world

Knowledge decides the depth. When Rayner and colleagues looked at who actually read fast with good understanding, the effective speed readers tended to be intelligent people who already knew a great deal about the topic. Their knowledge did the work the technique got credit for. The practical reading: on a subject you know well, an inspect may genuinely be enough, because you can tell a new claim from a familiar one at a glance. On a subject that is new to you, the same skim gives you headings you cannot yet judge, and the item needs a slower read.

Part 3 · covers elaborative interrogation as a reading move

Asking "why would this be true?" while you read

5 min live

Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan and Willingham (2013) reviewed ten learning techniques and rated each one. One of them is a question you can ask inside any text: elaborative interrogation, which means stopping at a stated fact and asking yourself why it would be true. They rated it moderate utility, and the reason it did not reach high is worth knowing as well as the technique.

LiveWhy would this be true? Moderate utility, and why not high3 min▶

Elaborative interrogation is simple to do while reading. When the text states a fact, stop and ask why it would be true, then try to answer from what you already know. Mina reads "Vendor A is cheaper below a certain monthly volume" and asks why. Her answer: a per-pick price scales with use, a per-seat price does not, so at low volume you pay little for picks while seats cost the same. Now the fact is attached to a reason, and she can spot when the reason stops holding.

Dunlosky and colleagues wrote: "We rate elaborative interrogation as having moderate utility." The moderate techniques fell short of high, in their words, because the evidence for their efficacy is limited. That is a statement about how much has been tested, across how many kinds of learners and materials, not a finding that the technique fails. Treat it as a good move with a thinner evidence base than retrieval, which is exactly how the review ranks it. Session 5 returns to it next to self-explanation, the other moderate technique built around asking yourself questions.

Self-studyWhen questions backfire3 min▶

A question written before reading directs attention. That is its job, and it is also its risk. Aim attention at one thing and you may read past the other thing that mattered. Mina's pricing question could carry her straight past p6 in the figure above, the paragraph on integration and middleware, which might decide the vendor choice on its own.

How large that cost is, the sources read for these pages do not say: no study of questions asked before reading was read at source, so no effect size is quoted here. Session 4 lets you see it on yourself instead. Its third trial gives you three of the five questions before you read, and the page splits your score into the questions you saw and the ones you did not. Two defences in the meantime: keep the during-questions from Part 2 running, because "what would change my mind?" is a question about what you were not looking for; and end every inspect by asking what the item covers that your question did not.

Real world

Mina's why-question finds a gap (constructed). Mina asks why per-seat pricing would ever win, and her answer depends on how many people use the software. The vendor report never states Harbourline's headcount for the picking floor. Her question has turned up the one number she now has to get from operations before Thursday, which no amount of rereading the report could have given her.

Build-along

You will write one question per item, sort each into skip, inspect or analyse with a minute budget, run one real inspectional pass, and leave with a plan for the one analytical read that matters. Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · As reported from Adler and Van Doren, what is inspectional reading?

Inspectional reading is the best you can do in limited time, and it is honest about getting a map rather than full understanding. A confuses it with speed reading, which session 1 showed trades comprehension for pace. C is the syntopical level. D is a shortcut, not the reported method.

2 · Mina has to choose a warehouse vendor by Friday. Which before-question best directs her reading of the vendor report?

A good before-question names the decision and what would settle it, so you recognise the answer when you see it. A and B have no answer you could recognise. C is a fair question but not the one her decision depends on. D also guards against the narrowing risk from Part 3.

3 · Why did Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) rate elaborative interrogation moderate rather than high utility?

The review says the moderate techniques fell short of high because the evidence for them is limited. That is a statement about how much testing has been done, not a finding that the technique fails, so B overcorrects. C and D are not reasons the review gives.
