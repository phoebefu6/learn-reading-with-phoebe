Learn Reading with Phoebe · Session 1 of 6

What reading speed really measures

A words-per-minute number looks like a measure of your eyes. It is mostly a measure of how fast you can recognise words and build meaning from them, and it only means anything next to a second number: how much you understood. Tonight we take the famous figures apart, from the 300 words per minute everyone quotes to the 238 a 190-study meta-analysis actually found, look at where the real bottleneck sits, and read what happened when speed-reading courses, one-word-at-a-time apps and the campaign against inner speech were put to the test.

Part 1 · covers the measured reading rate

The number everyone quotes, and the number that was measured

5 min live

Ask how fast adults read and someone will say 300 words per minute. Marc Brysbaert went looking for where that number came from and pooled 190 studies with 18,573 participants (Journal of Memory and Language, 2019). The average silent reading rate for adults in English came out at 238 words per minute for non-fiction and 260 for fiction, with most adults between 175 and 300 for non-fiction. Reading aloud averaged 183.

LiveWhat a words-per-minute number counts3 min▶

Words per minute is the number of words in a text divided by the minutes you spent on it. That is all. It says nothing about whether you understood, so on its own it rewards turning pages. Every serious study of reading rate measures a second number next to it, usually a comprehension test, and every claim on these pages pairs the two.

Two details make the published numbers comparable. Brysbaert's averages are for adults between 18 and 60 without reading problems, reading to understand the whole text. Non-fiction comes out slower than fiction mainly because non-fiction uses longer words: the gap is in the words, not in the reader. And his paper is blunt about the famous figure: setting a target of 300 words per minute is unrealistic for the majority of people.

Self-studyWhy your rate on a contract is not your rate on a novel3 min▶

A single "my reading speed" does not exist. Your rate moves with the words (long, rare, technical), with what you already know about the subject, and with what you are reading for. Rayner, Schotter, Masson, Potter and Treiman (Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2016) put the normal pace for most good readers at 200 to 400 words per minute, a range that wide because text and purpose vary that much.

Brysbaert also notes that rates measured when people skim for useful information are a different activity from reading the whole text, and should not be averaged with it. Someone who goes through 40,000 words of newspaper in an hour "reads" at 667 words per minute only in the sense that the arithmetic allows it. Session 2 turns this into a choice: decide what you are reading for, then decide the depth.

Real world

The figure that became a standard. Brysbaert describes 300 words per minute as one of the few numbers experimental psychology has given to society, and traces how often it is repeated without a source. When a reading app tells you that you read "below average" at 240, it is measuring you against that repeated number, not against the 190 studies.

Part 2 · covers what limits reading speed

The bottleneck is the words, not the eyes

5 min live

Reading looks like a smooth sweep along the line. It is a series of short stops, called fixations, with quick jumps between them, called saccades, and the occasional jump backwards, a regression. Rayner and colleagues' review is clear on what decides when the eyes move on: word identification. Language processing, not the ability to steer your eyes, is the primary driver of reading performance.

LiveWhy "see more per glance" does not work3 min▶

Speed-reading courses often promise you can take in a whole line, or a whole page, per glance. Rayner and colleagues put the limit plainly: the ability to read is limited by our ability to attend to, identify and understand words, rather than our ability to see them. Clear vision covers only a few letters around each fixation, so a wider sweep delivers words you cannot yet identify.

Regressions are the other target, and the review defends them. A jump back is more often the reader repairing a misunderstanding than the cause of one. Remove the chance to look back and you remove the repair.

Self-studyInner speech: a feature, not a leak3 min▶

"Stop saying the words in your head" is a standard instruction. Most skilled readers do hear something like an inner voice. The review summarises studies that suppressed it, for instance by having readers repeat a sound while reading, and reports that comprehension suffered when texts were reasonably difficult. Their conclusion is that inner speech is not a nuisance activity that must be eliminated. Even readers shown words one at a time at 720 words per minute appear to generate sound-based representations of them.

That is the contested claim, taught both ways: speed-reading teachers say subvocalising caps you at speaking speed; the research says the inner voice is part of how written words are held while a sentence is built, and suppressing it trades understanding for speed on anything hard. The evidence sits with the second view.

What actually makes people faster. The review's practical answer is unglamorous: read more, and read varied texts. Practice with language, a larger vocabulary and more knowledge of the subject speed up word identification, which is the step that sets the pace. Reading the same passage again and again does much less.

Part 3 · covers speed reading, skimming and RSVP

Faster costs understanding, and the bill is predictable

5 min live

The review's summary is one line: there is a trade-off between speed and accuracy. It is unlikely that readers can double or triple their speed, from around 250 to 500-750 words per minute, while understanding as well as at normal speed. Speed reading and skimming get you through faster with moderate comprehension. That last phrase is not a failure; for some reading it is exactly what you want.

LiveWhat happened when speed reading was tested3 min▶

The review collects the tests. After one speed-reading course (Calef, Pieper and Coffey, 1999), readers went from about 280 to about 400 words per minute and their comprehension fell from 81 to 74 percent correct. In Just, Masson and Carpenter's 1980 comparison, trained speed readers at 600 to 700 words per minute behaved much like people simply told to skim at that speed, and their advantage on gist vanished on harder Scientific American texts. The authors' reading: the students of speed-reading courses are essentially being taught to skim.

The effective speed readers in these studies tended to be intelligent people who already knew a lot about the topic. Knowledge is doing the work the technique gets credit for, which brings you back to Part 2: what you know speeds up word identification and meaning.

LiveRSVP apps: one word at a time2 min▶

Apps such as Spritz flash words one at a time in the same spot (rapid serial visual presentation, RSVP) so your eyes never move. The review found only two studies comparing such an app to normal reading. In one, Benedetto and colleagues (2015), readers going through a chapter of Orwell's 1984 read at about 200 words per minute with the app against 209 on the page, reported more eye fatigue and showed poorer literal comprehension. The review's conclusion is that RSVP apps will not let people double or triple their rate while keeping good comprehension. Removing eye movements also removes regressions, the repair move from Part 2.

Self-studyWhen skimming is the right tool3 min▶

Skimming rates can be two to four times faster than typical silent reading, with lower comprehension, and that is a fair trade when the goal is to find something or decide whether to read at all. The review describes what effective skimmers do: scan for headings, paragraph structure and key words to locate potentially relevant information, then read those parts more carefully. Readers who attend to headings write the most accurate summaries (Hyönä, Lorch and Kaakinen, 2002, as reported in the review), and skimming a whole text beat reading half of it normally for picking up its important ideas (Duggan and Payne, 2009, as reported).

So the rule is not "never skim". It is: skim on purpose, for a decision, and do not mistake the result for having read. Session 2 builds this into a two-speed habit, and session 4 lets you feel the trade on yourself.

Real world

Mina's app (constructed). Mina's app reported 520 words per minute on the vendor report's executive summary. When she tried to explain the vendors' pricing difference to her manager, she could not. Her app had measured one of the two numbers. The build-along below measures both.

Build-along

Your baseline, with both numbers

★ 22 min · everyone measures

Take a page you have to read this week anyway, about 400 to 800 words of non-fiction: a report section, a policy, an article for work. You will time one honest read, then check what stayed, and end with two numbers and one product of them. If you have nothing to hand, use any long-form news article. Mina uses page 4 of the vendor report.

Count the words without counting every word. Count the words on three typical lines, take the average, multiply by the number of lines. Within ten percent is plenty. If the text is digital, paste it into anything with a word count.

★ Step 1 · Mina's count3 sample lines: 11, 13, 12 words -> 12 per line Lines on page 4: 52 Words: 12 x 52 = 624 (word count tool says 611: close enough)

Read once, timed, to understand. Read the way you would if someone were going to ask you about it, because someone is. Start a stopwatch, read, stop it at the last word. Looking back within the page is fine; it is part of reading. A second full pass is not.

★ Step 2 · the timed readStarted 14:02:10, finished 14:05:01 Time: 2 min 51 s = 2.85 min Words per minute: 611 / 2.85 = 214

Close the page and write what stayed. Three sentences on what the page argued, then five specific facts: a number, a name, a condition, a cause, a recommendation. Do not peek. This is the comprehension half of the measurement and it is also, as session 3 shows, a learning move in its own right.

★ Step 3 · recall, book closedGist: Vendor A prices per pick, Vendor B per seat. A is cheaper under 40,000 picks a month. B's integration needs a middleware layer. Facts: 1) 40k picks/month break-even 2) B needs middleware 3) A's contract is 3 years 4) ? (pilot length) 5) ? (SLA)

Check against the text and score it. Score the three gist sentences as one point each if the page supports them, and each fact as one point. Write down what you got wrong as well as what you missed: a confident wrong fact is worse than a blank.

★ Step 4 · scoreGist: 3 of 3 supported Facts: 3 right, 2 blank (pilot is 6 weeks; SLA is 99.5%) Score: 6 of 8 = 75%

Compute the effective rate, and keep both numbers. Words per minute times the share you got right. It is a rough single figure for "understood words per minute", useful for comparing your own reads under different conditions, which is exactly what session 4 does. Compare your raw rate to the 175 to 300 band from Part 1, not to 300.

★ Step 5 · the two numbers, and one productRate: 214 wpm (inside the 175-300 non-fiction band) Comprehension: 75% Effective rate: 214 x 0.75 = 160 understood words per minute Your numbers will differ: this is Mina's constructed read.

Now try one skim, and compare. On a second page of similar difficulty, skim at roughly twice your rate, then do steps 3 to 5 again. Most people find the effective rate falls, but not always, and the cases where it holds up are the ones where you already knew a lot about the subject. Keep both results for session 4.

Homework

Before session 2

30 min
  1. Measure two more baselines on different kinds of text: one easy (a news feature), one hard (a technical document). Write down both numbers each time and notice how far your rate moves.
  2. Find the 300. Look for a reading-speed claim in an app, course or article. Does it say where the figure came from, and does it measure comprehension?
  3. Notice your regressions. On one hard paragraph, mark each time you looked back. Was each jump repairing something?
  4. Bring your pile. List everything you have to read in the next two weeks. Session 2 sorts it by what you need from each item.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Your app says you read non-fiction at 240 words per minute, "below the 300 average". What is the accurate reading?

Brysbaert (2019) found 238 for non-fiction and called a 300 target unrealistic for most people. A treats the eyes as the bottleneck, which the evidence does not support. D overcorrects: the rate is useful when it is paired with comprehension.

2 · According to Rayner and colleagues' review, what mainly decides how fast a skilled reader moves through a text?

The review says language processing, not eye control, is the primary driver, and that reading is limited by attending to, identifying and understanding words rather than seeing them. Suppressing inner speech and removing regressions both hurt comprehension on harder text.

3 · Mina has 40 minutes and a 60-page report. She only needs to know whether it covers integration costs. What does the evidence support?

Skimming is a fair trade when the goal is to locate something; effective skimmers use headings and structure, then slow down where it matters. RSVP did not deliver large gains with good comprehension, and suppressing inner speech hurts on difficult text.
