Learn Reading with Phoebe · Session 3 of 6

Rereading feels like learning

Read a page a second time and it goes down easier. The words look familiar, the argument flows, and that ease feels exactly like knowing it. Tonight we separate the two. In the best-known experiment, students who read a passage four times were the most confident of any group and remembered the least a week later. We look at why that happens, at how ten common study techniques were rated when the evidence was weighed, and at the one change that turns your notes from a second reading into a test: close the book first.

Part 0

Where we start

The running case is constructed. Mina, a new analyst at Harbourline (an invented shipping company), has read the 60-page vendor report twice and highlighted a good share of it. It feels known. On Friday she has to recommend Vendor A, which prices per pick, or Vendor B, which prices per seat, and say why out loud without the report in front of her. Tonight is about the gap between how well she feels she knows it and what she can actually produce, and the same gap sits under every page you have ever reread the night before a meeting.

Part 1 · covers the fluency illusion

Familiar is a feeling, recall is a fact

5 min live

When you reread, the text gets easier to process, and the brain reads that ease as a sign of learning. Researchers call it fluency. The trouble is that fluency is measured with the page open, and the test you actually face, in a meeting or an exam, happens with the page shut and days later. Roediger and Karpicke (2006) caught both signals in one experiment, and they point in opposite directions.

LiveFamiliarity is about the text, recall is about you3 min▶

Familiarity is a feeling you have while looking at the page: the sentences are easy, nothing surprises you, you could probably finish the next line. Recall is something you can do without the page: say the claim, give the number, explain why. Rereading builds the first very efficiently and the second much less, and because both feel like "knowing it", the cheaper one gets mistaken for the other.

The test is simple and slightly uncomfortable. Close the text and try to produce what it said. Whatever comes out is your recall; the gap between that and how sure you felt is the illusion, measured on yourself.

Live78% did better, 78% believed the opposite2 min▶

Kornell and Bjork (2008) had people learn the styles of 12 artists from their paintings. Some saw each artist's paintings in a block (massed); others saw the artists mixed together. In their paper the mixed schedule is called "spaced", though most researchers today would call it interleaved, and it is worth knowing both words because you will see both. In Experiment 1a the mixed schedule produced .61 correct on new paintings against .35 for the blocked one.

The striking part came afterwards. 78% of participants did better with the mixed schedule, and 78% said the blocked schedule had been as good or better. Blocked practice felt smoother, so people trusted it. The feeling and the result disagreed, and most people went with the feeling.

LiveFour reads: most confident, least remembered3 min▶

In Roediger and Karpicke's second experiment, students worked on prose passages in one of three patterns: read it four times (SSSS), read it three times and take one recall test (SSST), or read it once and take three recall tests (STTT). Tested five minutes later, more reading meant more recall: 83, 78 and 71 percent. Tested a week later, the order flipped: 40, 56 and 61 percent.

The SSSS group was also the most confident about how much they would remember, 4.8 on a 7-point scale against 4.2 and 4.0. They were rating the fluency they felt at the end of the session. At a week, the group with the most rereading had the lowest recall of the three. Their first experiment showed the same crossover between restudying and testing: 81 against 75 at five minutes, 42 against 56 at a week.

Self-studyStudents predicted the wrong winner3 min▶

Karpicke and Blunt (2011) compared retrieval practice, reading and then writing down everything you can recall, with building a concept map while the text was open. In their first experiment retrieval produced 0.67 a week later against 0.45 for concept mapping, and concept mapping was not significantly better than simply reading more. Before the results came in, students predicted that repeated studying would do best and retrieval worst.

In their second experiment, 101 of 120 students (84%) did better after retrieval practice, while 90 of 120 (75%) believed concept mapping had been as good or better. This is the fluency illusion in its most practical form: the method that feels productive while you do it is not the one that leaves the most behind.

Part 2 · covers what ten techniques were rated

The two techniques readers use most were rated low

5 min live

Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan and Willingham (2013) reviewed ten study techniques and rated each for how useful the evidence showed it to be across learners, materials and conditions. Two came out high. Three came out moderate, mainly because the evidence for them is still limited. Five came out low, and two of those five are the ones most readers rely on.

LiveThe ten ratings, and the reason behind each band3 min▶

High utility: practice testing and distributed practice. Both helped learners of different ages, on different materials, across different test delays. Moderate: elaborative interrogation (asking yourself why a stated fact is true), self-explanation (explaining how a new idea connects to what you know) and interleaved practice. The authors kept them out of the top band because the evidence for them "is limited", not because it pointed the wrong way. Low: summarization, highlighting and underlining, the keyword mnemonic, imagery for text learning, and rereading.

Their sharpest line is about the low band: "Most students report rereading and highlighting", and yet those two do not consistently boost performance. The techniques readers reach for first are the ones with the weakest evidence behind them.

Live"Low" is not "useless"3 min▶

A low rating says the benefit was small, inconsistent, or limited to some conditions. It does not say rereading does nothing. The same review notes that spaced rereading consistently beats massed rereading on delayed tests: reading something again after a gap does more than reading it twice in a row.

As reported in Dunlosky et al. (2013) and Greving and Richter (2019), Rawson and Kintsch (2005) found that rereading straight away helped on an immediate test, while rereading after a delay helped on a test two days later. That is the fluency illusion again: the massed version wins the test you take tonight and loses the one that matters. Callender and McDaniel (2009) are blunter; their abstract reports that "With only several exceptions, rereading did not significantly increase performance". So reread if you must, but reread later, and do not count it as your test.

Self-study"I am a visual learner": what the evidence says4 min▶

Learning styles are not in Dunlosky's ten, but they are the most common objection to everything above. It deserves care, because it is a disagreement between popularity and evidence rather than a settled joke. Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer and Bjork (2008) set out what a fair test of the popular "meshing" version would need: classify learners by style, randomly assign each group to instruction in each style, and test them all the same way. Meshing predicts a crossover: visual learners do best with visual teaching, verbal learners with verbal teaching. Better scores for one style overall would not count.

They found virtually no evidence of that crossover, and concluded there is "no adequate evidence base to justify incorporating learning-styles assessments into general educational practice". They also noted the limit of their own conclusion: not every possible version of learning styles has been tested. The fair summary is that the meshing version, as usually sold, has no adequate evidence base; people do have preferences, and matching instruction to them has not been shown to improve learning. Retrieval, by contrast, worked across the learners in the studies in Part 1.

Real world

Mina's yellow report (constructed). By Wednesday Mina had highlighted about 40% of the vendor report and reread the highlighted parts twice. Every page looked familiar. Her manager asked one question in the corridor: "Where's the break-even between A and B?" She knew it was in there, she could picture the table, and she could not name the number. Her highlights had told her where the answer was; nothing she had done had asked her to produce it.

Part 3 · covers turning a book into notes as retrieval

Close the book, then write

5 min live

Most notes are a second reading: you copy or paraphrase with the page open, and the act feels productive for exactly the reason rereading does. Swap the order and the same minutes turn into a recall test. Read a section, close it, write what you remember, then open it again to check and correct. That last step matters as much as the first; recall you never check can lock in a confident mistake.

LiveClose, write, check, correct3 min▶

The method is four moves. Read a section once, properly. Close it, or turn the screen away. Write what you remember: the claim, the evidence, the numbers, in your own words and in any order. Then open it and check line by line, correcting what you got wrong and adding what you missed in a different colour, so the next time you look you can see where your recall broke.

It is an old method. In the passage you will read in session 4, Benjamin Franklin describes teaching himself to write from the Spectator: he made short hints of each sentence, laid them by a few days, and then, "without looking at the book", tried to write the papers out again. Then he compared his version with the original and corrected his faults. Delay, recall without the text, check against the text, correct: the same loop, more than two centuries before anyone measured it.

LiveHighlighting as a pointer, not as learning2 min▶

You do not have to give up the highlighter. Change what it is for. A highlight is a pointer: it marks the sentence you will test yourself on later, the way a bookmark marks a page. It is not the learning, and colouring a line yellow does not move it into memory; it marks where to come back with a question.

The practical rule is to highlight sparingly and convert each highlight into a question soon after, the move the build-along practises. A page that is 40% yellow has no pointers at all, because everything is pointing.

Self-studyWhere the notes go next is a different job1 min▶

Tonight is about making a note by recall. Where those notes are filed, reviewed and found again is the job of a note system, covered in Learn PKM with Phoebe; linking notes to one another by idea is covered in Learn Obsidian with Phoebe.

Real world

Franklin and the Spectator. In his Autobiography (public domain; read the full text), Franklin explains that after rewriting a paper from his hints, he "compared my Spectator with the original, discovered some of my faults, and corrected them." He also jumbled his hints and, weeks later, put them back in order before writing, to teach himself method in arranging his thoughts. He was not studying for a test. He was using recall, delay and correction to get better at the thing itself.

Build-along

From highlights to five questions

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Take a page you have already highlighted: a report, a chapter, an article you marked up. If you have nothing highlighted, use the page you measured in session 1 and highlight five sentences now. You will turn each highlight into a question whose answer is the highlight, close the page, answer all five, then check and mark. Mina uses page 4 of the vendor report, the same page she timed in session 1.

Pick one highlighted page and choose five highlights. If the page has more, pick the five you would most need in a meeting: a number, a condition, a cause, a comparison, a recommendation. If it has fewer, highlight up to five now, sparingly.

★ Step 1 · Mina's five highlights (constructed)Vendor report, page 4: 1) A costs less than B below 40,000 picks a month 2) B's integration needs a middleware layer 3) A's contract term is 3 years 4) The pilot runs for 6 weeks 5) The SLA is 99.5% uptime

Turn each highlight into a question whose answer is the highlight. Write the question so that only the highlight answers it. "What about pricing?" is too loose; "Below how many picks a month is A cheaper?" has one answer. Write the answer on the back of a card, or below a fold in your notes, out of sight.

★ Step 2 · five questionsQ1 Below how many picks a month is Vendor A cheaper than B? Q2 What extra layer does Vendor B's integration need? Q3 How long is Vendor A's contract term? Q4 How long does the pilot run? Q5 What uptime does the SLA promise?

Close the page and answer all five. Turn the report over, close the tab, cover the backs of the cards. Answer every question, even with a guess, and mark how sure you were on each one before you look. A blank is information too.

★ Step 3 · answers, page closedA1 40,000 picks sure A2 middleware sure A3 3 years fairly sure A4 4 weeks? guess A5 ? blank

Check and mark. Open the page. Mark each answer right, wrong or blank, and correct the wrong ones in a different colour. Look hardest at any answer you marked "sure" that turned out wrong: that is the fluency illusion caught on your own page.

★ Step 4 · check against the pageA1 right A2 right A3 right A4 wrong -> pilot is 6 weeks (marked "guess") A5 blank -> SLA is 99.5% Score: 3 of 5 Your numbers will differ: this is Mina's constructed check.

Keep the questions, not just the score. Write the date, the source and the five questions somewhere you will find them. Session 5 puts these exact questions on a spacing schedule, so the work you did tonight becomes the first review rather than the last one.

★ Step 5 · kept for session 5Source: vendor report, page 4 Made: Wednesday Round 1: 3 of 5 (missed: pilot length, SLA) Next: answer again, page closed, on the schedule from session 5 Your numbers will differ.

Feel the difference once. Pick a second highlighted page and simply reread it for the same three minutes the questions took. Then, without looking, write what you can. Most people find the reread page felt easier and gives back less, but do not take that on trust; your own two pages are the evidence that counts, and session 4 lets you run the comparison properly.

30 min

  2. Catch one illusion. Before your next meeting or class, rate how well you know the material from 1 to 7, then write what you remember with the notes closed. Compare the rating with what came out.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · In Roediger and Karpicke's second experiment, students who read a passage four times were the most confident and had the lowest recall a week later. What best explains it?

The four-reads group led at five minutes (83 against 78 and 71) and rated themselves 4.8 of 7, yet scored 40 against 56 and 61 a week later. Confidence tracked the fluency they felt in the session, not the recall that lasted. C overcorrects: reading did raise immediate recall.

2 · Dunlosky and colleagues rated rereading and highlighting as "low utility". What does that rating mean?

Low is not zero. The review rated them low because they did not consistently boost performance, and it notes that spaced rereading outperforms massed rereading on delayed tests. The high band belongs to practice testing and distributed practice.

3 · What did Pashler and colleagues conclude about learning styles, and what would a valid test need?

Meshing predicts that each style does best with its own instruction, so the results must cross over. B claims more than the authors did; they said not every version was tested. D would show one method is better overall, which is not evidence for styles.
