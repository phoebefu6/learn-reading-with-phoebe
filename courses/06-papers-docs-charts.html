Reading a paper, a doc and a chart

Technical material punishes reading from the first word to the last. A research paper hides its claim in three places and its weakness in a fourth; documentation is a reference you enter with a task, not a story you follow; a chart makes its argument before you have checked what it is measuring. Tonight is the order of operations for each: Srinivasan Keshav's three passes and five Cs for a paper, task-first reading for a doc, and title, axes, units and baseline before the headline for a chart. Mina's Friday decision gets made on all three.

Part 1 · covers reading a research paper

Three passes, and permission to stop after the first

6 min live

Keshav's two-page note "How to read a paper" (ACM SIGCOMM Computer Communication Review, 2007) makes one move: read a paper in up to three passes instead of starting at the beginning and ploughing to the end. Each pass has a purpose, a time budget and a test of whether you are done. It is inspectional against analytical reading from session 2, applied to the genre where it pays the most.

LiveThe first pass, and the five Cs3 min▶

The first pass takes about five to ten minutes. Read the title, abstract and introduction carefully; read the section and subsection headings and ignore everything else; read the conclusions; glance over the references and tick the ones you recognise. Then answer the five Cs: Category (what type of paper is this?), Context (which other papers is it related to?), Correctness (do the assumptions appear to be valid?), Contributions (what are its main contributions?) and Clarity (is it well written?).

Keshav is explicit that you may choose not to read further after this. That is the point. He also turns it round for writers: most reviewers and readers will make only one pass, so the title, abstract, headings and conclusions have to carry the paper on their own.

Self-studyThe second and third passes3 min▶

Second pass, up to an hour. Read with greater care but skip details such as proofs. Look carefully at the figures and graphs: are the axes labelled, are there error bars, would the conclusion survive a different scale? Mark references you have not read. You are done when you can summarise the main thrust, with its supporting evidence, to someone else. That last test is retrieval from session 3 again: say it with the paper closed.

Third pass, about four or five hours for a beginner and about an hour for an experienced reader. Keshav's key is to attempt to virtually re-implement the paper: make the same assumptions and recreate the work, which exposes both its innovations and its hidden assumptions. At the end you should be able to rebuild the paper's structure from memory. Few papers at work need this; the one your decision rests on might.

Real world

Mina's two papers (constructed). Paper two was already skipped in session 2: its abstract showed it studied manual floors with no software. Paper one gets its first pass, eight minutes. It is a field trial in three warehouses (Category), and its Correctness question raises a flag: it compares pick-to-light against paper pick lists, not against handheld scanners like Harbourline's. That one line tells her which section to read carefully and what to discount. She gives section 4 and figure 2 a second pass and leaves the rest.

Part 2 · covers reading technical documentation

Enter a doc with a task, not at page one

5 min live

Documentation is a reference, not an argument. Reading it from the top is the analytical reading of session 2 spent on a text that was never meant to be read that way. The working practice is to arrive with a task, find the smallest piece that does it, run it, and read outward only as far as the task needs. This is practice, not a research finding: no study in the sources behind these pages measured documentation reading, so treat the steps as a method to test on your own work.

LiveFour steps into any doc3 min▶

1 · Write the task as a sentence. "Send yesterday's pick totals to the vendor's API and read back the error rate" is a task; "learn the API" is not. 2 · Find the one page that does it, through search or the reference index, and read only its parameters, one example and its error cases. 3 · Run the example, because a call that works is the comprehension check documentation allows, the equivalent of answering with the book shut. 4 · Read outward to the conceptual guide around that page only when something surprises you, and check the version and changelog date before trusting a detail.

The step most people skip is the first. Without a written task, the quickstart becomes the beginning of a cover-to-cover read, and an hour later you know the overview well and cannot make one call.

Self-studyWhen to read a doc the long way2 min▶

Task-first reading leaves gaps on purpose. When you will own a system for months, a slower analytical read of the conceptual guides once, early, pays back: it gives you the vocabulary that makes every later task-first search faster, which is session 1's point about knowledge speeding up reading. Turn what you learn into recall prompts and schedule them as in session 5; a doc you will use in three weeks is the clearest case for a spaced review.

Part 3 · covers reading a chart before believing it

Title, axes, units, baseline, source, and only then the headline

5 min live

A chart is read in a second and argued with for a week. Keshav's second pass already says to look carefully at figures, labels and error bars. The same order works for any chart someone hands you: read what it claims to measure before what it seems to show. Designing a chart that cannot mislead is a different skill and lives in the Data Visualization course ↗; here the job is reading one.

LiveFive reading steps for any chart3 min▶

Read in this order, out loud if it helps. 1 · Title: what exactly is being counted? "Picking errors" could mean wrong items, wrong quantities or both. 2 · Axes: what range is drawn, and is anything cut off? 3 · Units and denominator: per 1,000 orders, per shift, per picker? A rate and a count tell different stories when volume changes. 4 · Baseline: does a bar chart start at zero? If not, bar lengths no longer compare values. 5 · Source and period: whose data, which dates, and what changed in between? Only then read the headline, and check it against what you now know.

The five steps are a constructed checklist assembled for these pages, not a published standard. They are the chart version of Keshav's second-pass advice to look at axes and error bars, and of session 2's question before reading: here the question is "what would this chart have to show for the headline to be true?"

Self-studyDashboards, and the chart you see every week2 min▶

A dashboard chart is the opposite of a paper: you see it so often that it stops being read at all, which is session 3's fluency trap again. Once a quarter, run the five steps on the chart your team quotes most, as if you had never seen it. The units, filters and dates are where dashboards drift, because someone changed a definition and the picture stayed the same.

Real world

Mina's manager's chart (constructed). The dashboard headline says picking errors are tripling. Step 4 finds an axis starting at 4.0; steps 3 and 5 find the rate went from 4.3 to 4.9 per 1,000 orders between two quarters, while volume grew. A rise of about 14 percent is still worth a question for the vendors, and a very different question from "tripling".

Build-along

Mina's Friday read: a paper, a doc and a chart

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Bring one paper (or long report), one piece of documentation and one chart from your own work. If you have none, any open-access research paper, any public API's docs and any chart from a news site will do. Mina's constructed versions are in the boxes. You will leave with a one-page reading record for each.

First pass on the paper, eight minutes. Title, abstract, introduction; headings only; conclusions; glance at the references. Stop the timer and write the five Cs in one line each, then decide: stop, second pass, or third.

★ Step 1 · Mina, paper one (constructed)Category: field trial, 3 warehouses, 12 weeks Context: cites two earlier lab studies; none in software-run sites Correctness: compares pick-to-light against paper lists, not against scanners like ours: assumption to check Contributions: error rate fell after pick-to-light was introduced Clarity: clear; figure 2 has no error bars Decision: second pass on section 4 and figure 2 only

One task through the docs, eight minutes. Write the task first. Find the single page that does it, read its parameters, one example and its errors, then run or dry-run the example. Note the doc version and changelog date.

★ Step 2 · Mina, the vendor API (constructed)Task: send one day of pick totals, get back the error rate Page: POST /v2/picks/summary (found via the reference index) Read: 4 required fields, 1 example, 3 error codes Ran it: sandbox call returned 200 and an error_rate field Version: v2, changelog dated last month; v1 is deprecated Open question: is error_rate per order or per line?

The five chart steps, six minutes. Title, axes, units, baseline, source. Then rewrite the headline so it says only what the chart supports.

★ Step 3 · Mina, the dashboard chart (constructed)1 title: "picking errors" = wrong item OR wrong quantity 2 axes: y runs 4.0 to 5.0 3 units: per 1,000 orders; orders grew between quarters 4 baseline: not zero, so bar lengths overstate the change 5 source: warehouse system, Q1 and Q2 last year Headline, rewritten: "Errors rose from 4.3 to 4.9 per 1,000 orders (about 14%), with order volume also rising."

Write one record with three questions. One question per item, each answerable with the text closed. These go straight into the calendar from session 5: tomorrow, in a week, and before the meeting where you need them.

★ Step 4 · the recordQ1 What did paper one compare pick-to-light against, and why does that matter for us? Q2 Which endpoint returns the error rate, and is it per order or per line? (still open: ask the vendor) Q3 What did picking errors actually do between Q1 and Q2? Your answers will differ: these are Mina's constructed reads.

Where Mina lands (constructed). On Friday she recommends Vendor A, on the break-even she recalled in session 1, the field-trial evidence she read in two passes, discounted for its comparison, a working API call, and an error trend she can state without exaggeration. She read less of every item than she would have with the app, and she can explain all of it with the pages shut.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · After a ten-minute first pass, a paper's Correctness question fails: its key assumption does not hold where you work. What does Keshav's method say you may do?

Keshav says that after the first pass you may choose not to read further. The five Cs exist to make that decision in minutes. A spends an hour on a paper you have already judged unsuitable; C spends four.

2 · You need to make one API call by this afternoon. What is the most useful first move?

Documentation is a reference: a written task tells you which page matters, and a call that runs is the comprehension check. This is labelled practice, not a research result. Reading from the top is analytical reading spent on a text that was never meant to be read that way.

3 · A bar chart titled "Errors tripling" shows bars of 4.3 and 4.9 on an axis starting at 4.0. Which step in the five-step read catches it?

From a 4.0 baseline the bars are 0.3 and 0.9 tall, three times apart, while the values are 4.3 and 4.9. Units and source still matter: the rate is per 1,000 orders and volume rose. C overcorrects; the data can be fine and the drawing misleading.
