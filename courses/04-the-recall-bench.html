The recall bench

Three sessions of claims, tonight two benches. The first measures you: three short passages from public-domain books, a real clock, the text taken away, five questions. You read once at your normal pace, once skimming at double that pace, and once with three of the questions in hand before you start, and the page prints your own speed beside your own understanding. The second is a model, declared as one on the widget: twenty facts fading over thirty days, and what rereading tonight buys against a few minutes of recall spread across the month. It has an anti-lever that spends the most minutes, and a break button that proves the spacing is doing the work.

Part 1 · covers what the first bench measures

Three passages, three conditions, two numbers each

5 min live

Each trial is one passage, between 356 and 512 words, from three books in the public domain: Benjamin Franklin's Autobiography, Darwin's Voyage of the Beagle and Faraday's Chemical History of a Candle, with the text found on gutenberg.org. The page counts the words, starts the clock when the text appears and stops it when you press Done. Then the text disappears and five questions with four options each appear.

LiveThree numbers per trial3 min▶

Words per minute is the passage's word count divided by the minutes between the text appearing and you pressing Done. The counts are 356 words for Franklin, 454 for Darwin and 512 for Faraday, recounted by an independent Python run. Correct of 5 is your score on five questions, each with one supported answer in the text. Effective rate is words per minute times the share you got right: the rough "understood words per minute" from session 1.

The skim trial uses your trial 1 rate. If you read Franklin at 240 words per minute, the skim trial gives you 480, and the text disappears when that budget runs out. That is the "double or triple your speed" promise made concrete, on you.

Self-studyWhy this is a coarse instrument, and still worth running3 min▶

Five questions on one passage cannot estimate anyone's comprehension precisely. One lucky guess among four options moves the score by 20 points. The three passages also differ: Darwin's is full of island and species names, Faraday's is a spoken lecture, Franklin's is a short memoir. The rotation lets you put a different passage under each condition, and running the bench twice with two rotations gives you a crude counterbalance.

What the bench is good for is the thing apps leave out: you see your speed and your understanding in the same row, on your own reading, under conditions where the only change is the one named. The published studies in sessions 1 and 3 are the evidence; this is the feel.

Build-along · the first bench

Read three times, measured on you

★ 15 min · everyone reads

Run the trials in order; the skim needs your normal-pace rate first. Read for understanding, the way you would read something you will be asked about. Do not scroll back up to the questions while reading, and do not look at the text during the questions: the bench cannot stop you, and the only person fooled is you.

Read your results in this order. First the skim line: did doubling the speed lower the effective rate? Then the questions-first line: how many of the three questions you saw did you get, against the two you did not? Then the dots against the band of 175 to 300 words per minute, where most adults read non-fiction. Your numbers will differ from anyone else's, and from your own next week.

Part 2 · covers the forgetting model, declared

Twenty facts, thirty days, and a stability that grows

6 min live

Reading once is only half the problem; the other half is what is left in a month. The second bench is a model and says so. Each of 20 facts fades as R = exp(-t / S), where R is the chance you can recall it after t days and S, its stability, is how slowly it fades. It is the exponential family of forgetting models: Settles and Meeder's half-life regression, used at Duolingo, writes the same shape in base 2. Each review raises S, and raises it more when some forgetting has happened first.

LiveThe model, in four lines3 min▶

Twenty facts start with stabilities spread evenly on a log scale around five days, from easy to hard. A reread always succeeds and multiplies stability by 1 + (1 - R), then adds two days. A retrieval, recalling with the book closed and then checking, succeeds with probability R: on success stability is multiplied by 1 + 4 (1 - R) and two days are added; on failure you see the answer, which counts as a restudy from where you started. Day-30 recall is the exact average over every success and failure path, not a random run, so the same settings always print the same number.

Two honest limits. The settings are illustrative, fitted to nobody, so the ordering of schedules is the claim and the percentages are not. And the family is a simplification: the current FSRS scheduler uses a power-law curve, because it fits real review data better than a pure exponential.

LiveWhat the model prints3 min▶

Reading once and never reviewing leaves 1.0 percent recall at day 30. Rereading three more times that night, 40 minutes in all, leaves 7.6 percent. Three five-minute retrievals on day 1, 3 and 7, 25 minutes in all, leave 46.5 percent, and the same three retrievals on day 1, 7 and 21 leave 72.8 percent. Fewer minutes, more left, and the gap is not close.

The order flips on a shorter horizon. At day 14 the day 1/3/7 schedule leads, 78.5 against 67.5 percent, because its last review is closer. Stretch the gaps with the slider and watch day-30 recall climb, until at x1.50 the day 1/7/21 schedule pushes its last review to day 31.5, after the test, and falls to 37.3 percent. The best gap depends on when you need the material, which is exactly what the human data in Part 4 found.

Part 3 · covers the anti-lever and the break button

The schedule that spends the most minutes, and the gap set to zero

6 min live

A bench that can only print good news measures nothing. This one has two ways to print a worse number, and each is worth reading for what it shows about the model, not only about memory.

LiveThe anti-lever: reread five times tonight2 min▶

Five more passes the same evening: 60 minutes, more than double the spaced schedules. The model gives it 14.4 percent at day 30, against 46.5 and 72.8 for 25 minutes of spaced retrieval. It is the most effortful option and among the least effective.

The honest nuance, which the model keeps: rereading is not worthless. Five passes beat three, 14.4 against 7.6, and both beat reading once. Each extra pass adds a little stability, because the text is still fresh and a review at nearly full recall has little to repair. It is the least efficient minute you can spend, not a zero. That matches Dunlosky and colleagues' rating of rereading as low utility, not no utility.

LiveThe break button: set every gap to zero2 min▶

The break button takes the day 1/3/7 retrievals and runs them back to back tonight, ten minutes apart. If the advantage came from retrieval itself in this model, it would survive. It does not: day-30 recall falls to 7.7 percent, beside rereading's 7.6. Drag the slider to zero and you get the same. In this model the whole advantage comes from the gaps.

That is a claim about the model, and it is narrower than the human data. In Roediger and Karpicke's experiments, Part 4, testing beat restudy at a week even without days between reviews, so real retrieval carries a benefit of its own that this model leaves out on purpose to isolate spacing. The break button proves the bench measures spacing. It does not prove retrieval is useless when massed.

Real world

Mina's Friday (constructed). Mina has four days. She cannot do day 21, so she tries the slider: at x0.25 the day 1/3/7 schedule compresses to a few hours apart and the model's day-30 recall is 20.2 percent, well short of the 46.5 at full spacing. For a Friday meeting she needs day-4 recall, not day-30, and the model's lesson carries: put the last retrieval close to the meeting, and the first one tomorrow.

Part 4 · covers the human evidence behind both benches

What the experiments found, including what people predicted

5 min live

The model was built to behave like a body of experiments with real people. Three of them are worth knowing by their numbers, because each one also measured something the learner could not feel: that the strategy which felt better was worse.

LiveTesting beat restudy, and students predicted the opposite3 min▶

In Roediger and Karpicke's first experiment (Psychological Science, 2006), 120 students read short prose passages and then either read them again or took a recall test. Five minutes later, restudy was ahead, 81 against 75 percent recalled. Two days later the tested group was ahead, 68 against 54, and after a week 56 against 42. In the second experiment, four study periods (SSSS) against one study and three tests (STTT): 83 against 71 percent at five minutes, 40 against 61 percent at a week. The students who had only reread were the most confident they would remember.

Karpicke and Blunt (Science, 2011) found the same pattern against a more effortful rival: retrieval practice beat building a concept map, 0.67 against 0.45 on a test a week later, and 101 of 120 students did better after retrieval in the second experiment. Students again predicted the reverse.

LiveSpacing, and the gap that depends on the deadline3 min▶

Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted and Rohrer's 2006 synthesis pulled together 839 assessments of distributed practice from 317 experiments in 184 articles. Only 12 of 271 comparisons of massed against spaced study showed no effect or a negative one. Their 2008 study taught more than 1,350 people a set of facts and varied both the gap before a review and the delay to the test: the best gap was about 20 to 40 percent of a one-week delay and about 5 to 10 percent of a one-year delay. Interpolated, the best gaps for recall were about 3, 8, 12 and 27 days for tests at 7, 35, 70 and 350 days.

The model's day-14 against day-30 flip is that pattern in miniature. What these sources do not settle: whether gaps should widen (1, 3, 7) or stay even. The model rewards widening here only because its settings were chosen by hand, so treat "expanding is better" as an open question, not a finding.

Self-studyWhat the questions-first trial can and cannot tell you2 min▶

The bench splits your questions-first score into the three questions you saw and the two you did not. Questions before reading direct attention to what they ask about; whether that helps or costs you the rest of the text is exactly what the split shows, for you, on one passage. The bench claims nothing beyond that, and no study's effect size is quoted here, because none was read at source for these pages. Session 2's habit of writing your own questions is the version that does not depend on someone else choosing them.

The sentence to keep. "Rereading tells me the text is familiar. Recalling it with the book shut tells me whether I know it, and doing that again after a gap is what makes it stay."

Homework

Before session 5

30 min
  1. Run the first bench again with a different passage order. Did your skim cost the same? Write both effective rates side by side.
  2. Find your break-even. On the model, drag the slider for the day 1/7/21 schedule from x0.25 to x1.50 and note where day-30 recall peaks. Say in one line why it falls at x1.50.
  3. Plan one real retrieval. Pick something you read this week and must remember in a month. Put three recall sessions in your calendar, the first tomorrow.
  4. Predict, then check. Before your first recall session, write how much you think you will remember. Afterwards, compare. Bring the gap to session 5.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · On the first bench, your skim trial doubled your speed and your score fell from 4 of 5 to 2 of 5. What is the honest conclusion?

The direction fits Rayner and colleagues' speed-accuracy trade-off. The size does not generalise from one passage and five questions, where one question is 20 points. A claims a law nobody measured; C and D over-read a single noisy trial in opposite directions.

2 · In the model, the break button squeezes the day 1/3/7 retrievals into tonight and day-30 recall falls from 46.5 to 7.7 percent. What does that show?

The break button is a check on the instrument. A over-generalises it to people, against Roediger and Karpicke's finding that testing beat restudy at a week. C reads a modelled number as a measurement of real students.

3 · In Roediger and Karpicke's second experiment, which group was most confident it would remember the passage a week later, and how did it do?

Repeated study raised confidence and lowered week-later recall. The feeling of fluency from rereading is the subject of session 3, and the reason "it felt like I learned it" is not evidence.
